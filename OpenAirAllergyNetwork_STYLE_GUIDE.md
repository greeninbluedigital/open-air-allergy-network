# Open Air Allergy Network — Content Style Guide

This guide covers all written content for openairallergynetwork.com: directory/site copy and the
"Learn About ILIT" patient education articles. Its job is to fix one specific problem: the current
articles are accurate and well-organized, but they read as AI-smooth. This guide exists to name why,
and how to write around it, not to change the page structure or the underlying medical content, both
of which are working fine.

---

## Two Registers

**1. Site & directory copy** — service descriptions, provider profiles, how-the-directory-works
copy, navigation and calls to action. Voice: clear, credible, warm-professional. Speaks as the
platform, not as an individual. No first person.

**2. Ghostwritten patient articles** — the "Learn About ILIT" education content, bylined to a real
partner doctor (e.g. "By Dr. Sandra Ho, MD"). Voice: an actual clinician talking to a patient. This
needs to sound like something that doctor would put her name on and actually say in an exam room,
not marketing copy and not a generic AI health article. Authoritative because of real expertise,
warm because she's a doctor who wants to help. Address the reader as "you." Refer to "your allergist"
or "your provider" when discussing decisions outside the article's own scope.

If unsure which register a piece of content is, treat it as a ghostwritten patient article — that's
the higher-stakes register and the one this guide is mainly written for.

---

## The Smoothness Problem

Two sample articles were reviewed line by line. Both were accurate, well-structured, and needed only
minor edits, but both read as AI-smooth. Here's specifically why, so future drafts can be checked
against the same patterns.

### 1. The recycled hedge-closer

The same underlying move, reworded, appeared five-plus times across two short articles:

- "though coverage varies by plan, so it's worth confirming with your own provider"
- "Coverage varies by plan... confirm specifics with your own insurer"
- "ask your allergist what a specific course would look like for your allergens"
- "It's a conversation to have directly with your allergist"
- "Ask your allergist whether transitioning mid-course... fits your situation"

**Deferring to a patient's own doctor is correct here, both medically and legally. Don't stop doing
it.** The problem is saying it the same way, in the same sentence shape, over and over. It reads as
templated because it is templated.

**Fix:** each deferral should carry a genuine, specific reason attached to it, not a bare pointer.
Vary the sentence shape every time. If an article needs to defer to a provider more than twice,
that's a sign the article itself is being too vague and should say more concrete things instead.

### 2. Rule-of-three and rule-of-four overload

- "how treatment is given, how long it takes, and how often you're in a clinic"
- "your specific allergens, schedule, insurance situation, and how you feel about needles versus a
  longer at-home routine"
- "insurance copays, gasoline, parking, and prescription or over-the-counter antihistamines"

Balanced three- and four-item lists dressed as prose are a well-documented AI rhythm default.
**Fix:** vary it. Two items. One standalone point. A specific example instead of a list.

### 3. FAQs that just re-paraphrase the body

Body copy: "The buildup phase is weekly or biweekly visits as the dose increases, typically taking
three to six months."

FAQ answer on the same topic: "The buildup phase, when your dose gradually increases, typically
takes three to six months, and many people notice some improvement during that time."

The FAQ adds exactly one new fact (improvement during buildup) after restating everything already
said in the body. **Fix:** an FAQ answer must lead with something not already stated in the body, or
it should be cut. FAQs get read by people who skip straight to them, and get pulled out individually
by search engines — a redundant one wastes that placement.

### 4. Nothing specific enough to stick

No named guideline body (AAAAI, ACAAI), no cited study, no real number beyond broad ranges like
"three to five years" or "a handful of visits." Everything is qualitatively accurate but nothing is
concrete enough to be memorable, checkable, or worth citing elsewhere. See Medical Sourcing below —
the fix here is real, not invented.

### 5. Uniform paragraph rhythm

Nearly every paragraph in both articles follows the same shape: a general claim, then a softening
qualifier clause. No short paragraph, no blunt one-line statement, no length variation anywhere.
**Fix:** let a paragraph be two sentences sometimes. Let one sentence per section be short and land
flat, without a qualifier attached.

## Medical Sourcing and Claims

- **Never invent a statistic, percentage, or study finding.** If a specific number is used (a
  success rate, a timeframe, a cost figure), it must come from something real and citable — a named
  professional body (AAAAI, ACAAI), a published guideline, a peer-reviewed study — or be explicitly
  framed as the reviewing physician's own clinical experience or opinion ("In Dr. Ho's practice,
  most patients notice...") rather than stated as a population-level fact.
- **No absolute or guarantee language.** Avoid "cures," "eliminates," "guaranteed," "risk-free,"
  "always works." Immunotherapy outcomes vary by patient. Say that accurately, once, rather than as
  a reflexive hedge on every sentence.
- **No personalized medical advice.** Articles inform; they don't diagnose or prescribe. Pointing a
  reader to "talk with your allergist" is appropriate — see the hedge-closer fix above for how to say
  it without it becoming a tic.
- **Every ghostwritten article should read like something the attributed doctor actually reviewed and
  would put her name on.** If a claim sounds like it needs backup, either back it up or cut it.
- **No fear-based or exaggerated framing to drive signups.** Confidence should come from real
  expertise, not manufactured urgency about allergy risk.

## Anti-AI-Tell Rules (baseline, applies to both registers)

**Hard bans:** delve, tapestry, navigate, landscape, robust, leverage (as a verb), unlock, elevate,
seamless, comprehensive, "it's important to note," "in today's world."

**Banned transitions:** Moreover, Furthermore, Additionally, In conclusion, "That said" as a
paragraph opener.

**No hyphens, em dashes, or semicolons as a crutch for joining clauses.** Use a period or a
conjunction instead.

**Vary sentence length aggressively.** A run of uniform medium-length sentences is the clearest
sign of AI-written text.

**No rule-of-three lists** (see above — this was the single most common issue found).

**No throat-clearing opener and no tidy summary-paragraph closer.**

---

## Structure Rules

- **Lead with the point.** The first sentence of an article, and of each section, should answer the
  question the heading implies, before adding supporting detail.
- **Keep the existing page template.** Intro, mechanism, what to expect, comparison, FAQ, CTA is a
  clear, sensible structure for patient education. The fix here is at the sentence and paragraph
  level, not the page architecture — don't restructure pages to solve this problem.
- **FAQs must be non-redundant** with the body, per the rule above.
- **Keep bylines and "last reviewed" dates prominent.** That's a real trust signal for medical
  content and should stay exactly as-is.

## Worked Examples

**Hedge-closer, before:**
"Coverage varies by plan. SCIT (allergy shots) has the longest track record of the immunotherapy
options and is the one most commonly covered — confirm specifics with your own insurer."

**After (same information, not templated, adds a real reason):**
"SCIT has decades of track record, so it's the option insurers cover most often. ILIT is newer and
typically self-pay today. Coverage rules shift, so check your specific plan rather than assuming
either way."

**FAQ redundancy, before:**
"The buildup phase, when your dose gradually increases, typically takes three to six months, and
many people notice some improvement during that time."

**After (leads with the new fact, doesn't restate the body):**
"Many patients notice some improvement during the three-to-six-month buildup phase itself, before
reaching full maintenance dosing."
