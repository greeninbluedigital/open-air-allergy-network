# Title
Allergy Shots & Allergy Immunotherapy: A Full Guide

# Meta Description
Allergy shots build long-term tolerance to allergens over three to five years. Learn how they work, what to expect, and faster options like ILIT.

# Tags
Patient Guide, ILIT, SCIT

# Key Takeaways
- Allergy shots (SCIT) are a well-established form of allergy immunotherapy given over three to five years.
- Treatment introduces small, increasing amounts of allergen so your immune system builds long-term tolerance.
- Most people start with weekly or biweekly visits, then taper to a monthly maintenance schedule.
- ILIT is a newer immunotherapy option that can compress treatment into months instead of years.
- You don't have to choose ILIT from the start — ask your allergist whether transitioning mid-course is an option for you.

# Body

Allergy immunotherapy is the only category of allergy treatment that changes how your immune system responds to an allergen over time, rather than just controlling symptoms as they come up. Allergy shots — subcutaneous immunotherapy, or SCIT — are the original and most established form of it, used for decades.

## How Allergy Shots Work

Each shot contains a small, carefully measured amount of the allergen you react to, injected just under the skin. Over repeated visits, the dose gradually increases, training your immune system to tolerate the allergen instead of overreacting to it. This is different from antihistamines or nasal sprays, which manage symptoms after they start rather than addressing the underlying reaction.

## What to Expect

Allergy shots happen in two phases. The buildup phase is weekly or biweekly visits as the dose increases, typically lasting several months. Once you reach an effective maintenance dose, visits taper to monthly, continuing for a total of three to five years. Every visit ends with a short waiting period so your allergist can monitor for a reaction.

That multi-year timeline is also the most common reason people hesitate to start, or don't finish once they do.

## A Faster Option: ILIT

Intralymphatic immunotherapy (ILIT) is a newer approach built around the same basic idea — introducing allergen so your immune system builds tolerance — but delivered differently. Instead of repeated under-skin injections over years, ILIT delivers allergen extract directly into a lymph node, guided by ultrasound, typically over a handful of visits across a few months rather than years.

ILIT isn't the right fit for every allergy profile — it's built around a defined set of common allergens — and it's currently generally self-pay rather than insurance-covered, unlike SCIT. But it's worth knowing about whether you're deciding how to start allergy treatment for the first time, or you're already partway through allergy shots and wondering if there's a faster path from where you are. Ask your allergist whether starting ILIT, or transitioning to it mid-course, fits your situation — many providers offer both.

# FAQ

Q: How long do allergy shots take to work?
A: Most people start noticing symptom improvement during the buildup phase, but the full course — buildup plus maintenance — typically runs three to five years for lasting, long-term tolerance.

Q: What happens during an allergy shot appointment?
A: You receive an injection containing your specific allergen(s) at a measured dose, then wait in the office for a short period afterward so your allergist can monitor for any reaction.

Q: Can I switch from allergy shots to ILIT partway through treatment?
A: It's a conversation to have directly with your allergist — many providers offer both SCIT and ILIT and can advise whether transitioning mid-course makes sense for your specific situation.

Q: Are allergy shots covered by insurance?
A: Coverage varies by plan. SCIT has the longest track record of the immunotherapy options and is the one most commonly covered — confirm specifics with your own insurer.

Q: Is ILIT a type of allergy immunotherapy?
A: Yes — like allergy shots, ILIT works by introducing allergen so your immune system builds tolerance over time. The difference is delivery method and timeline, not the underlying approach.
