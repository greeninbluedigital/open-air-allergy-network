Both tests look for the same thing: IgE, the antibody your immune system makes against a specific allergen, like grass pollen or cat dander. A skin test finds it by watching how your skin reacts. A blood test measures it in a lab.

Skin testing is usually where allergists start. It's fast, and the [American College of Allergy, Asthma and Immunology](https://acaai.org/allergies/testing-diagnosis/) (ACAAI) notes that blood tests take longer and cost more. Some people are better off with a blood test, though, and the reasons are specific.

## Why testing comes before immunotherapy

Every form of immunotherapy, whether shots, drops, or ILIT, treats specific allergens. Your treatment is built from your test results, so the test is the foundation, not a formality.

## How a skin prick test works

A tiny drop of each possible allergen is pricked or scratched into the surface of your skin. According to the ACAAI, placing the allergens takes about 5 to 10 minutes. Then you wait about 15 minutes to see whether a small, itchy red bump appears where an allergen went in.

You get your results before you leave.

If a prick test comes back negative but your symptoms still point to a particular allergen, your allergist may try an intradermal test, where a small amount of the allergen goes just under the skin with a thin needle.

## How an allergy blood test works

A blood test takes one standard blood draw. [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/lab-tests/allergy-blood-test/), from the National Library of Medicine, says drawing the sample usually takes less than five minutes. The lab then measures IgE to each allergen your allergist ordered, and results come back later rather than during your visit.

## When a blood test makes more sense

The most common reason is medication. If you take something that interferes with skin testing and can't safely stop it, a blood test sidesteps the problem.

Skin conditions are the other big one. The [ACAAI](https://acaai.org/resource/allergy-testing/) lists eczema and psoriasis, which can make skin hard to test or read.

There's also a small group of people so sensitive to an allergen that even the tiny exposure in a skin test could be risky. For them, a blood test is the safer route.

## The antihistamine question

Antihistamines block the reaction a skin test looks for, so they can make a real allergy look negative. The ACAAI advises stopping most of them about a week before testing, with some variation by medicine. Hydroxyzine needs 10 days, while diphenhydramine (Benadryl) only needs to be stopped 48 hours ahead. Some psychiatric medicines can interfere too.

Don't stop a prescription on your own to get ready. The clinic can tell you which of your medicines matter and whether a blood test would be simpler.

## A positive result isn't a diagnosis

This surprises a lot of people. A positive skin test means your immune system has made IgE to that allergen. It doesn't guarantee you'll have symptoms when you're around it, and the ACAAI says you may not react at all. Blood tests can also show false positives.

That's why an allergist reads test results together with your history, such as when your symptoms flare and what seems to set them off. Two people with the same positive test can need very different treatment plans.

## Which test is right for you?

For most people, a skin prick test is the natural first step. A blood test is the backup when skin testing can't give a clear or safe answer.

Either way, the result is a starting point for the conversation with your allergist, not the end of it. If you're considering ILIT, [find an ILIT provider near you](/find-an-ilit-provider) to talk through testing and whether ILIT fits your allergens.
