KEY TAKEAWAYS
- ExACT Immunoplasty, QUICK-LI, RapImmune, A-ILIT and the other names are practices' names for one procedure, intralymphatic immunotherapy (ILIT).
- Most programs describe three ultrasound guided injections about four weeks apart, finished in about eight weeks.
- A-ILIT uses lymph nodes in the neck. Most other programs use a lymph node in the groin.
- The allergy guidelines say ILIT should be given only by specialists trained in ultrasound guided lymph node injections.
- A brand name doesn't change the evidence: every program rests on the same ILIT research.

FAQS
Q: Is ExACT Immunoplasty the same as ILIT allergy treatment?
A: Yes. ExACT Immunoplasty is one practice group's name for intralymphatic immunotherapy (ILIT), and its materials describe the standard ILIT course: three ultrasound guided injections into a lymph node in the groin, four weeks apart. The [2026 allergen immunotherapy practice parameter](https://college.acaai.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/2026-Allergen-Immunotherapy-Practice-Parameter-Update.pdf) from the AAAAI and ACAAI describes ILIT the same way, as typically three injections into a superficial lymph node, usually finished within 8 weeks. If a practice uses a brand name, ask which lymph node and how many allergens its version includes.

Q: Where can I get ExACT Immunoplasty near me?
A: ExACT Immunoplasty, QUICK-LI and the other names here are offered only by the practices that use them, but the treatment behind them, intralymphatic immunotherapy (ILIT), is offered under its plain name too. The [2026 allergen immunotherapy practice parameter](https://college.acaai.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/2026-Allergen-Immunotherapy-Practice-Parameter-Update.pdf) notes that most practicing allergists aren't trained in ILIT, so it helps to search for the treatment rather than one brand. Use [Find a Provider](/find-an-ilit-provider) on OpenAirAllergyNetwork.com to locate an allergist who offers it.

Q: What is the ExACT Immunoplasty success rate?
A: The research cited in the allergy guidelines studies ILIT itself, not a brand name, so the evidence for any brand is the evidence for ILIT. The longest follow-up, a [19-year study](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacig.2022.09.007) of patients from the first ILIT trial in Switzerland, found that 19 of the 25 ILIT patients who answered its survey (76%) reported lasting improvement and were satisfied with the treatment, about the same as patients who had allergy shots. Only 25 of the 58 people originally treated with ILIT answered, so it's a small sample. Figures on a practice's own website are that practice's claims, so ask where any number comes from.

Q: Does an immunoplasty shot hurt?
A: Most ILIT studies describe it as a well tolerated procedure. In a [2008 trial in Switzerland](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2582048/) that compared ILIT with standard allergy shots, people treated with ILIT found the lymph node injection significantly less painful than a needle stick into a vein, according to the [2026 allergen immunotherapy practice parameter](https://college.acaai.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/2026-Allergen-Immunotherapy-Practice-Parameter-Update.pdf). If needles worry you, ask the practice how long the injection itself takes and what you'll feel during it.

Q: Does insurance cover branded lymph node allergy shots?
A: Usually not, and the brand name doesn't change that. [Ohio State University Wexner Medical Center](https://health.osu.edu/health/general-health/ilit-allergy-shots) says patients pay out of pocket for ILIT because it hasn't been approved by the FDA, and our [Learn page explains](/learn-about-ilit#faq-fda) that the extracts are FDA approved while the lymph node route is off label. Ask the practice what its program fee covers, and see our [HSA and FSA answer](/learn-about-ilit#faq-hsa-fsa) for paying with pretax funds.

Q: Do ILIT allergy injections treat food allergies?
A: No. The [2026 allergen immunotherapy practice parameter](https://college.acaai.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/2026-Allergen-Immunotherapy-Practice-Parameter-Update.pdf) describes ILIT as injections of aeroallergen extract, meaning airborne allergens such as pollen, dust mites, pet dander and mold. Practices listed on OpenAirAllergyNetwork.com that offer branded programs describe the same environmental allergens, and one says plainly that food allergies aren't treated. For a food allergy, ask an allergist about the options for that allergy specifically.

Q: Is QUICK-LI the same as ExACT Immunoplasty?
A: They're two practices' names for the same procedure, intralymphatic immunotherapy (ILIT). Practices listed on OpenAirAllergyNetwork.com describe both as three ultrasound guided injections into a groin lymph node, four weeks apart, over about eight weeks. Details such as the observation time and how many allergens go into one series differ by practice, so compare those when you call.

Q: Is RapImmune the same as Rapamune?
A: No. RapImmune is a practice's name for intralymphatic immunotherapy (ILIT), an allergy treatment. Rapamune is the brand name for sirolimus, which [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/druginfo/meds/a602026.html) lists as an immunosuppressant used with other medicines to prevent kidney transplant rejection. If a search for RapImmune keeps showing Rapamune, add "allergy" to the search.

ExACT Immunoplasty, QUICK-LI, RapImmune and the other names on allergy practice websites all describe one treatment: intralymphatic immunotherapy, or ILIT allergy treatment, which puts small doses of allergen extract into a lymph node. The [2026 allergen immunotherapy practice parameter](https://college.acaai.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/2026-Allergen-Immunotherapy-Practice-Parameter-Update.pdf), the joint guideline from the American Academy of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology (AAAAI) and the American College of Allergy, Asthma and Immunology (ACAAI), describes ILIT as typically three injections into a superficial lymph node, one just under the skin, using ultrasound guidance, with the course usually finished within 8 weeks. Each practice's name sits on that same procedure, though details such as which lymph node is used differ between programs.

Whichever name brought you here, the treatment behind it is one form of allergy immunotherapy, alongside allergy shots and allergy drops or tablets, and its short schedule makes it a faster alternative to allergy shots.

## Why practices give ILIT allergy shots their own names

"Intralymphatic immunotherapy" runs 11 syllables, and "ILIT" means little to most patients. A name of its own gives a practice something shorter to say and something unique to market. The [U.S. Patent and Trademark Office](https://www.uspto.gov/trademarks/basics/what-trademark) (USPTO) says a trademark identifies the source of goods or services, and calls a mark used for services a service mark. Anyone claiming a mark can write ™ or ℠ without filing an application, the USPTO says, while ® may be used only after the USPTO registers the mark.

None of these programs describes a different medicine. Each one describes ILIT as allergen extract injected into a lymph node. The extracts themselves are FDA approved, while injecting them into a lymph node is off label, as our [Learn page explains](/learn-about-ilit#faq-fda). The 2026 guideline recommends that your allergist tell you when a treatment is off label and note it in the consent form.

## What is ExACT Immunoplasty?

ExACT Immunoplasty℠, often searched as "immunoplasty", is one practice group's name for ILIT. Its materials describe three injections four weeks apart, eight weeks in all, given with ultrasound guidance into an inguinal lymph node, in the groin. The injection takes about 5 to 10 minutes and is followed by 60 minutes of observation.

The program starts with an allergy test covering up to 58 allergens, and the first treatment can be scheduled the same day as the test. Up to 9 allergens can go into one treatment series. Patients bring an epinephrine auto-injector, such as an EpiPen, on treatment days, and people who take a beta blocker are asked to have their doctor stop it at least 24 hours before each treatment. The [ACAAI](https://acaai.org/resource/allergy-testing/) says an allergist decides about stopping a beta blocker only after talking with your cardiologist or primary care doctor, so never stop one on your own.

## What is QUICK-LI?

QUICK-LI®, also written Quick-LI, is a name more than one practice uses for ILIT. Practices listed on OpenAirAllergyNetwork.com describe it as three ultrasound guided injections into an inguinal lymph node in the groin, four weeks apart and finished in about eight weeks. Patients wait 30 to 60 minutes after each injection, depending on the practice, and some practices recommend taking your usual allergy medicines on treatment day. People who take beta blockers are asked to tell their doctor.

## What is RapImmune?

![Body outline marking ILIT injection sites at lymph nodes on both sides of the neck and in the groin](https://res.cloudinary.com/qruprn0t/image/upload/v1795575980/ilit-brand-names-injection-sites-lymph-nodes-neck-groin-diagram.jpg "Most ILIT programs use a lymph node in the groin. A-ILIT uses lymph nodes in the neck.")

RapImmune® is a practice's name for ILIT allergy injections, not a drug. Google's search suggestions sometimes turn it into Rapamune, the brand name for sirolimus, which [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/druginfo/meds/a602026.html) lists as an immunosuppressant used to prevent kidney transplant rejection. The two are unrelated.

RapImmune's materials describe three visits over about eight weeks, with ultrasound guidance into a lymph node at the top of the thigh, near the hip crease. The first visit includes a review of your allergy testing and a mapping of the injection site.

## What is A-ILIT? Lymph node allergy shots in the neck

A-ILIT, short for Advanced Intralymphatic Immunotherapy, is the one program here that uses lymph nodes in the neck. Its materials describe three ultrasound guided injections into cervical (neck) lymph nodes, given at the start, at four weeks and at eight weeks.

Most of the other programs describe a lymph node in the groin, as does [Ohio State University Wexner Medical Center](https://health.osu.edu/health/general-health/ilit-allergy-shots), which says its ILIT injections go into the inguinal lymph nodes in the groin just under the skin. The 2026 guideline doesn't pick a site. It says only "a superficial lymph node". If the injection site matters to you, ask which node a practice uses before you book.

## How ILIT programs differ, and what to ask

- **Training and ultrasound.** The guideline recommends that only specialists trained in ultrasound guided lymph node injections give ILIT, in a setting ready to treat a serious reaction. It cites a European training program where, before formal training, only 58 of 156 injections reached the lymph node, and hitting the node was linked to how well the treatment worked. Ask who gives the injections and how much ILIT experience they have.
- **Spacing.** The guideline says injections every 4 weeks, rather than every 2, contribute to ILIT's effectiveness and safety. Most programs here use about four weeks. Ask the schedule for all three visits before you start.
- **How many allergens.** The guideline says the evidence supports treating one allergen, with very limited evidence for up to three at once. Ask how many allergens a program puts into your series and how it chooses them.
- **Which lymph node.** Most programs use the groin, and A-ILIT uses the neck.
- **Testing.** Some programs test and treat on the same day. Others review earlier results first, so ask what the first visit includes.

## What the research says about ILIT allergy shots

The research behind every brand is ILIT research. The [2026 guideline](https://college.acaai.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/2026-Allergen-Immunotherapy-Practice-Parameter-Update.pdf) calls ILIT one of the newest forms of allergen immunotherapy, with randomized trials reported in 23 publications, and says clinical access in the U.S. emerged during the COVID-19 pandemic. That research is growing but smaller than the evidence behind allergy shots: the guideline dates human ILIT studies back about a quarter century, against more than 100 years of allergy immunotherapy use overall. Results vary from person to person.

A [2008 trial in Switzerland](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2582048/) gave adults with grass pollen allergy either ILIT or standard allergy shots. In the guideline's summary, the ILIT group had fewer serious side effects and rated the injection as less painful than a needle stick into a vein. The guideline also describes relief within two months of finishing the three injections and improvement lasting at least 3 years. A [19-year follow-up](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jacig.2022.09.007) of patients from that first ILIT research surveyed them again: 19 of the 25 ILIT patients who answered (76%) reported lasting improvement and were satisfied with the treatment, about the same as the allergy shot group. Only 25 of the 58 people originally treated with ILIT answered, so it's a small sample.

Serious reactions to ILIT are rare, the guideline says. Many practices still ask you to bring an epinephrine auto-injector, such as an EpiPen, to every treatment visit and watch you for a while after each injection. Ask your allergist about the risks for your allergens, including severe reactions. Practice websites sometimes publish their own success rates. Those numbers are the practice's claims, so ask where any figure comes from.

## How do you find ILIT allergy treatment near you?

Search for the treatment rather than one brand name. A branded program is offered only by the practices that use that name, and the 2026 guideline notes that most practicing allergists aren't trained in ILIT. With three injections over about eight weeks, ILIT is the closest thing to a fast allergy treatment schedule that immunotherapy offers.
