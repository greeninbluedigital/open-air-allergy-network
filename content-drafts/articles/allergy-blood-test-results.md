KEY TAKEAWAYS
- Allergy blood test results are reported in kilounits per liter (kU/L), on a scale of about 0.1 to 100.
- One national lab flags 0.35 kU/L and up as high and calls 0.10 to 0.34 a result of undetermined clinical relevance.
- A higher number or class means your body is more likely to recognize the allergen, but it doesn't show how severe the allergy is.
- About 50% to 60% of positive allergy blood tests don't turn out to be an allergy.
- A normal total IgE doesn't rule out an allergy, so read the numbers for each allergen.

FAQS
Q: What does kU/L mean on an allergy test?
A: It stands for kilounits per liter, the unit labs use for the amount of IgE antibody in your blood. [Thermo Fisher](https://corporate.thermofisher.com/content/dam/phadia/library/en/Quick_guide_to_using_ImmunoCAP_Specific_IgE_testing.pdf), which makes the ImmunoCAP blood test, describes it as kilounits of allergen-specific IgE per liter of blood, sometimes written kUA/L. The [AAFA](https://aafa.org/allergies/allergy-diagnosis/) says results are reported on a scale of 0.1 to 100 kilounits.

Q: What does 0.35 kU/L mean on an allergy test?
A: On a common class scale, 0.35 is the bottom of class 1. A [sample report from ARUP Laboratories](https://www.aruplab.com/Testing-Information/resources/HotLines/Sample_Reports/Aug2019/3001729_Allergen%20Region%2019%20Respiratory%20Panel%20IgE_REG19PANEL.pdf) labels 0.35 to 0.70 kU/L as low and flags 0.35 as above its normal range of 0.34 or less. [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/treatments/22345-allergy-blood-test) notes that scoring varies from one test brand to another, so read it against your own lab's range.

Q: What does a class 2 allergy mean?
A: On ARUP Laboratories' [class scale](https://www.aruplab.com/Testing-Information/resources/HotLines/Sample_Reports/Aug2019/3001729_Allergen%20Region%2019%20Respiratory%20Panel%20IgE_REG19PANEL.pdf), class 2 covers 0.71 to 3.50 kU/L and is labeled moderate. The [AAFA](https://aafa.org/allergies/allergy-diagnosis/) says the class doesn't reflect how severe an allergy is. Whether a class 2 result matters depends on whether you have symptoms around that allergen.

Q: What is the difference between total IgE and specific IgE?
A: Total IgE is the overall amount of IgE antibody in your blood, and specific IgE is measured one allergen at a time. [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/lab-tests/allergy-blood-test/) says a high total can mean you have some kind of allergy but doesn't show what you're allergic to. [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/treatments/22345-allergy-blood-test) calls the specific test the more sensitive of the two.

Q: Can you have high IgE but no allergies?
A: Yes. [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/lab-tests/allergy-blood-test/) says smoking or a parasitic infection can also raise IgE. The [ACAAI](https://acaai.org/allergies/testing-diagnosis/) says blood and skin test results alone don't diagnose allergies and have to be read together with your medical history.

Q: Can you have allergies with normal IgE?
A: Yes, especially if only total IgE was measured. [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/treatments/22345-allergy-blood-test) says many people with subtle allergies may have a normal total IgE level. In its [interpretation guide](https://corporate.thermofisher.com/content/dam/phadia/library/en/Quick_guide_to_using_ImmunoCAP_Specific_IgE_testing.pdf), Thermo Fisher says about 30% of patients show raised allergen-specific IgE with a normal total, which is why it doesn't recommend screening with total IgE.

Q: How accurate are allergy blood tests?
A: Positive results are the weak spot. [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/treatments/22345-allergy-blood-test) says about 50% to 60% of positive allergy blood tests don't turn out to be an allergy, while [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/lab-tests/allergy-blood-test/) calls false negatives uncommon. The [ACAAI](https://acaai.org/allergies/testing-diagnosis/) says skin testing is the method allergists prefer and is usually the most accurate.

Q: Can a positive allergy blood test lead to allergy shots?
A: It can, when the positive allergens match your symptoms. The [ACAAI](https://acaai.org/allergies/testing-diagnosis/) says allergy shots contain a tiny but increasing amount of the allergen you're sensitive to, and the [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/allergy-shots-%28immunotherapy%29) says maintenance shots typically continue for three to five years. ILIT (intralymphatic immunotherapy) is typically three injections over about two months. To ask whether your results fit it, use [Find a Provider](/find-an-ilit-provider) on OpenAirAllergyNetwork.com to locate an allergist who offers it.

When your allergy blood test results come back, each allergen you were tested for usually gets a number in kilounits per liter (kU/L), and some labs add a class from 0 to 6 based on that number. The [Asthma and Allergy Foundation of America](https://aafa.org/allergies/allergy-diagnosis/) (AAFA) says these IgE allergy test results run on a scale of 0.1 to 100, and a higher number means your body is more likely to recognize that allergen. A positive result shows sensitization. An allergist calls it an allergy when it matches the symptoms you get around that allergen.

Whichever allergens show up on your report, the same list later shapes treatment. Allergy shots, allergy drops and ILIT (intralymphatic immunotherapy), which some people choose as a faster alternative to allergy shots, all work on the specific allergens you react to.

## How to read allergy blood test results

A typical report lists each allergen tested, a number beside it and the lab's normal range. [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/treatments/22345-allergy-blood-test) says results can take a few days to come back.

1. **Check the units.** Allergen-specific IgE is reported in kU/L, sometimes written kUA/L. [Thermo Fisher](https://corporate.thermofisher.com/content/dam/phadia/library/en/Quick_guide_to_using_ImmunoCAP_Specific_IgE_testing.pdf), which makes the ImmunoCAP test, describes the unit as kilounits of allergen-specific IgE per liter of blood. Your lab order may call the test ImmunoCAP, CAP or RAST, names used by the AAFA and [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/lab-tests/allergy-blood-test/).
2. **Compare each number with your lab's range.** On a [sample report from ARUP Laboratories](https://www.aruplab.com/Testing-Information/resources/HotLines/Sample_Reports/Aug2019/3001729_Allergen%20Region%2019%20Respiratory%20Panel%20IgE_REG19PANEL.pdf), a national reference lab, the normal range beside each allergen reads 0.34 kU/L or less, and a 0.35 is flagged high. Thermo Fisher counts 0.1 or more as a sign of sensitization, so the 0.10 to 0.34 band is a gray zone that ARUP leaves to specialists.
3. **Look for a class column.** Some reports turn each number into a class. The chart below shows one lab's version.
4. **Find total IgE.** It sits on its own line with its own range. On the ARUP sample, the adult patient's range was 214 kU/L or less.
5. **Match each positive to your symptoms.** The [American College of Allergy, Asthma and Immunology](https://acaai.org/resource/allergy-testing/) (ACAAI) says allergy tests confirm what your health history already tells the allergist. Its example: a positive dust mite test only matters if you sneeze or get itchy eyes when you vacuum or stir up house dust.

## Allergy blood test results chart: classes 0 to 6

ARUP Laboratories prints this scale on its allergen reports:

| Class | Specific IgE (kU/L) | ARUP's label |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Less than 0.10 | No significant level detected |
| 0/1 | 0.10 to 0.34 | Clinical relevance undetermined |
| 1 | 0.35 to 0.70 | Low |
| 2 | 0.71 to 3.50 | Moderate |
| 3 | 3.51 to 17.50 | High |
| 4 | 17.51 to 50.00 | Very high |
| 5 | 50.01 to 100.00 | Very high |
| 6 | More than 100.00 | Very high |

Your report may not match it exactly. Cleveland Clinic explains that labs use different brands of allergy blood tests, so the scoring varies from brand to brand. Go by the ranges printed on your own report.

## Total IgE vs. specific IgE

Specific IgE is the test behind each allergen's number. MedlinePlus says a separate test is run for each allergen, while total IgE measures all the IgE in your blood at once.

Cleveland Clinic says total IgE doesn't show what you're allergic to, and that many people with subtle allergies may have a normal total. Thermo Fisher's guide puts a number on it: about 30% of patients show raised specific IgE with a normal total, so the company advises against screening with total IgE. A high total can also come from causes other than allergy. MedlinePlus names smoking and parasitic infections.

Thermo Fisher adds that normal total IgE depends on age, so read yours against the range your lab prints for your age.

## What does a positive allergy blood test mean?

A positive allergy blood test means your immune system has made IgE to that allergen. The AAFA says that doesn't necessarily mean the allergen caused your symptoms, and it calls the blood test a poor screening tool because of its high false positive rate. Cleveland Clinic says about 50% to 60% of positive results don't turn out to be an allergy.

That's why the ACAAI says blood and skin test results alone don't diagnose allergies. When results are unclear or don't fit your history, allergists rely on the history and an exam to make the final call.

### Does a higher number mean a worse allergy?

It means your body is more likely to recognize the allergen, but it doesn't measure severity. The AAFA says the score and class don't reflect how severe an allergy is, and that no skin or blood test can tell how severe it is. ARUP's report adds that higher levels may not match how strongly you react to the allergen. Only a supervised challenge test, where you take a tiny, slowly increasing amount of the allergen, can show severity, according to the AAFA.

## Negative allergy blood test but still have symptoms?

MedlinePlus calls false negatives uncommon. Still, ARUP's report notes that a negative test may not rule out an allergy, so a negative with clear symptoms is worth taking back to your allergist.

Your allergist may order a skin test, which the [ACAAI](https://acaai.org/allergies/testing-diagnosis/) calls the preferred and usually most accurate method. Or the cause may not be allergic at all: the ACAAI's testing Q&A says people without an identifiable trigger could have a nonallergic nasal condition. If you're weighing the two tests, our [allergy skin test vs. blood test](/blog/allergy-skin-test-vs-blood-test) guide covers when each one fits.

## Component testing for food allergies

For some foods, a second blood test can sort out a confusing positive. The ACAAI describes a peanut component test that shows which peanut proteins your IgE targets. Ara h 1, Ara h 2 and Ara h 3 carry more risk than others, and the results help an allergist decide whether to do an in-office food challenge. Cleveland Clinic notes that certain component tests can predict severity, which standard blood tests usually don't show. If you're at risk of anaphylaxis, MedlinePlus says you may need to carry an epinephrine auto-injector, so ask your provider whether that applies to you.

## Longer term options

Your blood test results become a list of allergens to treat. Thermo Fisher's guide suggests ranking them from highest to lowest, cutting exposure to the highest ones first and focusing on indoor allergens, since those may be easier to control. Allergy medicine eases symptoms day to day. Immunotherapy goes after the allergy itself, and the ACAAI says shots contain a tiny but increasing amount of the allergen you're sensitive to.

**Allergy shots (SCIT)** start with a build-up phase of shots once or twice a week for four to six months, then maintenance shots every two to four weeks for three to five years, according to the [American Academy of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/allergy-shots-%28immunotherapy%29) (AAAAI).

**Allergy drops or tablets (SLIT)** go under the tongue. The [ACAAI](https://acaai.org/allergies/testing-diagnosis/) says immunotherapy under the tongue, like shots, uses gradually increasing doses of your allergen.

**ILIT (intralymphatic immunotherapy)** is the shortest of the three courses, for people who want a fast allergy treatment schedule. An allergist injects FDA approved allergen extract into a lymph node in the groin (just below the waist, near the hip), an off label route, typically three times over about two months. Research is promising but smaller than for allergy shots, and results vary. The [Learn About ILIT](/learn-about-ilit) page covers who may be a candidate.

If you just got your blood test results, an allergist who offers ILIT can go over them with your history and tell you which allergens a short course could treat. [Find an ILIT allergy treatment provider near you](/find-an-ilit-provider) to start that conversation.
