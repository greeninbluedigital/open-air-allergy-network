KEY TAKEAWAYS
- Cottonwood fluff is the tree's seed carrier, and extension experts in Colorado and Ohio say there's no evidence it causes allergies.
- Cottonwood pollen comes from male trees in early spring, weeks before female trees shed their cotton.
- If you sneeze when the cotton flies in June, grass pollen is a likely suspect, since grass pollinates from April through June.
- An allergy skin or blood test can tell cottonwood pollen apart from grass and other trees.
- If medicine isn't enough, immunotherapy treats the allergy itself, and ILIT is the shortest course, about three injections over about two months.

FAQS
Q: Can cottonwood trees cause allergies?
A: Yes, but through their pollen, not their fluff. Cottonwood is on the [AAFA's](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/aafa-2026-allergy-capitals-report.pdf) list of trees that cause the most allergy symptoms in the U.S., and [Thermo Fisher's allergen fact sheet](https://www.thermofisher.com/allergy/wo/en/allergen-fact-sheets/cottonwood-tree.html) calls its pollen moderately allergenic. The pollen comes from male trees in early spring, before any cotton appears.

Q: When is cottonwood allergy season?
A: Cottonwoods typically pollinate between February and May, according to [Thermo Fisher](https://www.thermofisher.com/allergy/wo/en/allergen-fact-sheets/cottonwood-tree.html), and [Colorado State University Extension](https://planttalk.colostate.edu/?p=2172) puts the pollen in April in Colorado. The cottonwood fluff season comes later, in June, after the pollen is done.

Q: What are cottonwood allergy symptoms?
A: They're the usual hay fever symptoms: sneezing, a stuffy or runny nose, watery eyes, and an itchy throat and eyes, says [Thermo Fisher](https://www.thermofisher.com/allergy/wo/en/allergen-fact-sheets/cottonwood-tree.html). Some people also wheeze, and tree pollen may trigger or worsen asthma. Nothing about the symptoms points to cottonwood in particular, so the season they show up in is a better clue.

Q: Can cottonwood cause a sore throat?
A: Its pollen can make your throat itch, according to [Thermo Fisher's cottonwood fact sheet](https://www.thermofisher.com/allergy/wo/en/allergen-fact-sheets/cottonwood-tree.html), along with your nose and eyes. The fluff itself isn't an allergen, [Colorado State University Extension](https://planttalk.colostate.edu/?p=2172) says. Our guide to [whether allergies can cause a sore throat](/blog/can-allergies-cause-sore-throat) covers postnasal drip and how to tell allergies from an infection.

Q: When is cottonwood season in Michigan?
A: Usually in June, sometimes starting in late May. On June 1, 2023, [FOX Weather](https://www.foxweather.com/earth-space/video-cottonwood-tree-shed-east-grand-rapids) reported cottonwood cotton turning lawns in East Grand Rapids white, like snow flurries, and a Michigan State University forester told [CBS Detroit](https://www.cbsnews.com/amp/detroit/news/snowing-in-june-nuisance-cottonwood-seeds-return-to-metro-detroit) the "snowfall" usually lasts a week or two. Grass pollen is in the Michigan air from April through June, says [Blue Cross Blue Shield of Michigan](https://www.bcbsm.mibluedaily.com/stories/prevention/when-is-allergy-season). Our [Michigan providers page](/find-an-ilit-provider/michigan) lists allergists in the state who offer ILIT.

Q: Is cottonwood a poplar?
A: Yes. [Ohio State University Extension](https://bygl.osu.edu/node/2644) places cottonwoods in the genus *Populus*, the poplars, and Thermo Fisher's fact sheet calls them poplars too. That matters for allergies because [Thermo Fisher](https://www.thermofisher.com/allergy/wo/en/allergen-fact-sheets/cottonwood-tree.html) says people with a cottonwood pollen allergy may also react to poplars and willows.

Q: What is the treatment for a cottonwood allergy?
A: Allergy medicine, especially a nasal steroid spray, eases symptoms, and the [AAFA](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/aafa-2026-allergy-capitals-report.pdf) suggests starting a couple of weeks before your season. If medicine doesn't give complete relief, immunotherapy treats the allergy itself. ILIT (intralymphatic immunotherapy) is the shortest course, typically about three injections over about two months. To try it, use [Find a Provider](/find-an-ilit-provider) on OpenAirAllergyNetwork.com to locate an allergist who offers it.

For anyone with a cottonwood allergy, or anyone who starts sneezing the week the white fluff drifts across lawns and piles up along the curb, the cotton looks like the obvious culprit. It usually isn't. Cottonwood fluff is the tree's seed carrier, and extension experts say there's no evidence it triggers allergies. The pollen that can cause symptoms comes weeks earlier, and when the cotton flies in June, grass pollen is usually in the air too.

Whichever pollen is behind your June sneezing, knowing which one it is decides what helps: when to start medicine, which allergens to test for, and whether to treat the allergy itself with allergy shots, allergy drops or ILIT, a faster alternative to allergy shots.

## Is cottonwood fluff an allergen?

No. [Colorado State University Extension](https://planttalk.colostate.edu/?p=2172) says plainly that the cotton "has no allergenic properties," and Joe Boggs of [Ohio State University Extension](https://bygl.osu.edu/node/2644) wrote in May 2026 that he could find no evidence that cottonwood fluff triggers allergic reactions.

The cotton is part of how the tree spreads its seeds. Cottonwoods grow as separate male and female trees. The female trees form small capsules that split open in June and release seeds attached to white, cottony fibers, which carry them on the wind. [New Mexico State University Extension](https://nmsudesertblooms.blogspot.com/2021/04/two-myth-busters-1-is-cottonwood-fluff.html) describes the cotton as the seed with a fuzzy appendage to help it fly. In a heavy year it can turn lawns white, and Ohio State notes it can clog air conditioners, car radiators and pool filters.

## Cottonwood allergy season: the pollen comes first

Cottonwood pollen comes from the male trees, whose catkins release it on the wind in early spring to reach the female flowers, Ohio State explains. Colorado State puts that pollen in April in Colorado, and says the April pollen is what causes problems for allergy sufferers. [Thermo Fisher's cottonwood fact sheet](https://www.thermofisher.com/allergy/wo/en/allergen-fact-sheets/cottonwood-tree.html) gives a wider window, February to May, and calls the pollen moderately allergenic.

Cottonwood is on the [Asthma and Allergy Foundation of America's](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/aafa-2026-allergy-capitals-report.pdf) list of trees that cause the most allergy symptoms in the U.S., along with poplar, aspen and willow. You won't see the pollen that bothers you. The AAFA explains that wind-carried tree pollen is small and light, and it's the type most people are sensitive to. Big, visible pollen is heavy and falls near the tree.

So if your nose runs in March or April, cottonwood is one of the trees that could be responsible. If your worst weeks line up with the cotton in June, look elsewhere.

## What's actually in the air when the cotton flies

Colorado State says people with symptoms during the June cotton are probably reacting to other wind-borne pollen, from grasses, weeds or other trees. Grass is the usual suspect. The [AAFA](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/aafa-2026-allergy-capitals-report.pdf) says grass causes most late spring and summer pollen allergy symptoms, from April through early June, and that grass season can overlap with tree season.

In Michigan, the two overlap. On June 1, 2023, [FOX Weather](https://www.foxweather.com/earth-space/video-cottonwood-tree-shed-east-grand-rapids) reported cottonwood cotton turning lawns in East Grand Rapids white, like snow flurries, and a Michigan State University forester told [CBS Detroit](https://www.cbsnews.com/amp/detroit/news/snowing-in-june-nuisance-cottonwood-seeds-return-to-metro-detroit) that the "snowfall" usually lasts a week or two. [Blue Cross Blue Shield of Michigan](https://www.bcbsm.mibluedaily.com/stories/prevention/when-is-allergy-season) says grass pollen is in the Michigan air from April through June, while tree pollen runs from as early as February through late spring. Our [Michigan allergy season guide](/blog/michigan-allergy-season) has the full month by month calendar.

Farther north, grass can run later. Dr. Lee Frelich of the University of Minnesota's Center for Forest Ecology told [FOX 9](https://www.fox9.com/news/cottonwood-seeds-minnesota-season-sneeze) in June 2024 that the Twin Cities grass pollen season gets going in early June and peaks in late June and early July. "You're not allergic to cottonwood right now," he said.

## Cottonwood allergy symptoms

A cottonwood pollen allergy looks like any other hay fever. Thermo Fisher lists sneezing, a stuffy or runny nose, watery eyes, and an itchy throat and eyes, plus wheezing in some people. Tree pollen may also trigger or worsen asthma. The [American Academy of Allergy, Asthma and Immunology](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/mowing-down-your-grass-allergies) describes the same nose and eye symptoms for grass allergy, which is why the calendar alone can't settle it.

Thermo Fisher says many people with a cottonwood allergy also react to other tree, weed or grass pollens, and may react to poplars and willows. A skin prick test or a blood test can show which pollens you're actually sensitized to. Our guide to [allergy skin tests vs. blood tests](/blog/allergy-skin-test-vs-blood-test) explains how each works.

## How to get through cottonwood and grass season

These steps come from the [AAFA's 2026 report](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/aafa-2026-allergy-capitals-report.pdf).

1. **Start medicine before your season.** The AAFA suggests starting allergy medicine a couple of weeks before your season begins. For cottonwood pollen, that can mean late winter. For grass, which the AAFA says starts in April, it means around March. Nasal steroid sprays are the most effective type for nose symptoms, and many are sold over the counter.
2. **Keep the windows closed at peak times.** The AAFA says pollen usually peaks in the afternoon. Run central air or an air cleaner with a HEPA filter if you have one.
3. **Leave pollen at the door.** Take off your shoes, wash the clothes you wore outdoors, and dry laundry in the dryer or on an indoor rack instead of a line.
4. **Shower before bed.** It washes pollen off your body so it doesn't end up in your bedding.

## Longer term options

Allergy medicine treats the symptoms. If it doesn't give you complete relief, the AAFA suggests talking with an allergist about immunotherapy, which can change how your immune system responds to the pollen.

**Allergy shots (SCIT)** are the longest course. The [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/allergy-shots-%28immunotherapy%29) describes shots once or twice a week for four to six months, then every two to four weeks for three to five years. Our [guide to allergy shots](/learn-about-ilit/allergy-shots-and-allergy-immunotherapy) walks through the schedule.

**Allergy tablets and drops (SLIT)** dissolve under the tongue. The AAAAI says a tablet is approved for grass allergy in the U.S. Our [comparison of allergy shots, allergy drops and ILIT](/learn-about-ilit/allergy-shots-vs-allergy-drops-vs-ilit) covers which allergens each one treats.

**ILIT (intralymphatic immunotherapy)** is the shortest course. An allergist uses ultrasound to inject FDA approved allergen extract into a lymph node, usually in the groin (just below the waist, near the hip). Giving the extract that way is an off label use. For people whose June symptoms turn out to be grass, the main study is a close match: a [2008 trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2582048/) randomized 165 people with grass pollen allergy to either 54 allergy shots over three years or three lymph node injections over two months. Tolerance to grass pollen in the nose rose within four months in the ILIT group, and after three years it was comparable to the shot group, with fewer side effects.

ILIT research is promising but smaller than the research behind allergy shots, and results vary from person to person. The [ILIT FDA FAQ](/learn-about-ilit#faq-fda) explains the off label question.
