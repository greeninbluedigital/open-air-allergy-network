KEY TAKEAWAYS
- About one in four U.S. adults has a seasonal allergy, according to a CDC survey.
- Tree pollen leads in early spring, grass in late spring and summer, and ragweed and other weeds from late summer through fall.
- Winter symptoms often come from indoor allergens such as dust mites, pet dander and mold, sealed in with you when the windows are shut.
- Nasal steroid sprays are the most effective single class of medicine for hay fever, per the ACAAI, and many allergy medicines work best started before the season.
- Allergy shots can bring long-term relief over 3 to 5 years, and ILIT is a shorter course of about three injections over two months, with a smaller body of research.

FAQS
Q: Is hay fever the same as seasonal allergies?
A: Mostly, yes. The [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/outdoor-allergens-ttr) says seasonal allergic rhinitis is the medical name for what's commonly called hay fever. The [ACAAI](https://acaai.org/allergies/allergic-conditions/hay-fever/) uses "hay fever" for year-round allergic rhinitis too, and points out that it doesn't take hay to get it and it doesn't cause a fever.

Q: How long do seasonal allergies last?
A: As long as you're exposed to the allergen, according to [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/hay-fever/symptoms-causes/syc-20373039), so symptoms can last as long as a pollen you react to is in the air. [MedlinePlus](https://magazine.medlineplus.gov/article/what-triggers-seasonal-allergies) notes that, unlike a cold, seasonal allergies usually last longer than a week or two.

Q: Can you develop seasonal allergies as an adult?
A: Yes. [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/can-develop-allergies-later-in-life) says most allergies begin in childhood, but adult-onset allergies are most common in the 30s and 40s and can appear at any age. An allergist with the [ACAAI](https://acaai.org/resource/can-allergies-develop-later-in-life/) says a move to a new environment, a new pet or a change of job can be among the triggers.

Q: Do seasonal allergies go away with age?
A: Sometimes, but don't count on it. [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/can-develop-allergies-later-in-life) says childhood allergies often fade, while allergies that show up later in life tend to stick around. Treatment can change the picture: [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/tests-procedures/allergy-shots/about/pac-20392876) says some people have no allergy problems after a few years of successful allergy shots, even once the shots stop.

Q: Can seasonal allergies cause itchy skin?
A: They can. The [ACAAI](https://acaai.org/allergies/allergic-conditions/hay-fever/) lists itchy eyes, mouth or skin among the signs of allergic rhinitis, and says antihistamines can help relieve itchy skin and hives as well as nasal symptoms.

Q: Can seasonal allergies trigger asthma?
A: Yes. [MedlinePlus](https://magazine.medlineplus.gov/article/what-triggers-seasonal-allergies) says many people with seasonal allergies also have asthma, and pollen can trigger shortness of breath, wheezing and coughing. [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/hay-fever/symptoms-causes/syc-20373039) lists worsening asthma as a complication of hay fever, and its allergy shots page names seasonal allergic asthma among the conditions shots can help control.

Q: What is the best medicine for seasonal allergies?
A: For nasal symptoms, the [ACAAI](https://acaai.org/allergies/allergic-conditions/hay-fever/) calls steroid nasal sprays (intranasal corticosteroids) the single most effective class of drug for allergic rhinitis, since they ease congestion, sneezing, itching and a runny nose. Antihistamines are also commonly used. If you're unsure about a specific medicine, the ACAAI suggests checking with your allergist or pharmacist.

Q: Why are allergies so bad right now?
A: Weather plays a part. The [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/outdoor-allergens-ttr) says each plant pollinates at about the same time every year, but the weather affects how much pollen is in the air, and pollen travels more in hot, dry, windy weather. Seasons are also getting longer. A [2021 study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33558232/) of 60 North American pollen stations found pollen seasons arriving earlier and lasting longer from 1990 to 2018, with 21% more pollen in the air.

Q: Can intralymphatic immunotherapy treat seasonal allergies?
A: Grass pollen allergy was the focus of an early trial of intralymphatic immunotherapy. In a [2008 trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19001265/) of 165 people, three injections into a lymph node over two months gave tolerance three years later about equal to 54 allergy shots over three years, with fewer side effects. If you'd like to ask whether it fits your allergies, use [Find a Provider](/find-an-ilit-provider) on OpenAirAllergyNetwork.com to locate an allergist who offers it.

Seasonal allergies are the sneezing, stuffy nose and itchy eyes that show up at the same times each year, when pollen and mold spores fill the air, and they're common: in a [CDC survey](https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/products/databriefs/db460.htm), 25.7% of U.S. adults had a diagnosed seasonal allergy in 2021. What sets them off depends on the season. Trees usually lead in spring, grasses follow, weeds take over in late summer and fall, and winter symptoms tend to come from allergens inside your home.

Whichever season gets to you, the options run from allergy medicine for the worst weeks to treatments that change the allergy itself: allergy shots, allergy drops and tablets, and ILIT (intralymphatic immunotherapy), a faster alternative to allergy shots.

## When to get care right away

Pollen can trigger asthma, causing shortness of breath, wheezing and coughing, according to [MedlinePlus](https://magazine.medlineplus.gov/article/what-triggers-seasonal-allergies). The [National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/asthma/attacks) says to call 911 if your medicines aren't relieving an asthma attack or breathing is still very hard.

## What causes seasonal allergies

A seasonal allergy is your immune system treating a harmless pollen or mold spore as an invader. The [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/outdoor-allergens-ttr) (American Academy of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology) explains that the body makes antibodies called IgE against it, and those antibodies set off the release of chemicals that cause the symptoms. [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/hay-fever/symptoms-causes/syc-20373039) names histamine as one of them.

The AAAAI says showy flowers such as roses usually don't cause allergies, because insects carry their pollen. The trouble comes from plants whose flowers make powdery pollen that spreads easily on the wind.

Mayo Clinic lists having other allergies or asthma, having eczema, and having a parent or sibling with allergies or asthma among the risk factors for seasonal allergies.

## When is allergy season?

Allergy season generally runs from early spring into fall, with a different pollen leading at each stage. The [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/outdoor-allergens-ttr) says each plant's pollen period changes little from year to year, while the weather decides how much pollen is in the air on a given day. In warmer places, pollination can go on all year.

| Season | Main trigger | Also in the air or at home |
|---|---|---|
| Early spring | Tree pollen, especially birch, oak, elm and maple | Outdoor mold spores start rising as it warms |
| Late spring into summer | Grass pollen | Mold spores keep climbing, peaking in July in warmer states |
| Late summer and fall | Ragweed and other weed pollen | Outdoor mold, peaking in October in colder states |
| Winter | Indoor allergens: dust mites, pet dander, mold, cockroaches | Mold spores year-round in the South and on the West Coast |

Some people react in every column. The [American College of Allergy, Asthma and Immunology](https://acaai.org/allergies/allergic-conditions/hay-fever/) (ACAAI) says people can have both seasonal and year-round allergic rhinitis, with their year-round symptoms getting worse during certain pollen seasons.

## Spring allergies: tree pollen

Tree pollen is a leading cause of spring allergies, and it starts early. The AAAAI places it in early spring, and [MedlinePlus](https://magazine.medlineplus.gov/article/what-triggers-seasonal-allergies) names birch, oak, elm and maple in particular. Grass pollen joins in by late spring, and the ACAAI lists tree and grass pollen as the most common spring triggers. Our [tree pollen allergy guide](/blog/tree-pollen-allergy) covers which trees cause the most trouble and how long their season lasts.

## Summer allergies: grass pollen and mold

Grass pollen is a main summer allergy trigger. Mayo Clinic says it's common in late spring and summer, and the AAAAI says it often causes symptoms in late spring and early summer.

Outdoor mold spores build through the same months: they increase as temperatures rise in spring, and in warmer states they reach their peak in July, according to the [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/outdoor-allergens-ttr). Pollen tends to travel more on hot, dry, windy days, which can make symptoms worse, while rainy, cloudy or still days often bring milder symptoms.

## Fall allergies: ragweed and outdoor mold

Ragweed is a major fall allergy trigger. [MedlinePlus](https://magazine.medlineplus.gov/article/what-triggers-seasonal-allergies) says it releases pollen from late summer through fall, and the [ACAAI](https://acaai.org/allergies/allergic-conditions/hay-fever/) adds other weed pollens and outdoor mold to the fall list. In colder states, the AAAAI says outdoor mold spores peak in October, and it suggests leaving the leaf raking to someone else, since raking stirs up pollen and mold. Our [ragweed allergy guide](/blog/ragweed-allergy) covers that season in detail, and the [mold allergy guide](/blog/mold-allergy) explains spore counts and symptoms.

## Winter allergies: indoor allergens

Winter allergies often come from indoors. The [ACAAI](https://acaai.org/news/do-you-have-the-winter-sniffles-5-ways-you-can-manage-indoor-winter-allergies/) says indoor allergies are a year-round problem but people notice them more in winter, because they spend more time at home with the doors and windows shut, sealing allergens in while the heating system recirculates the air. The indoor triggers it names include dust mites, pet allergens, cockroach allergens and mold, which the ACAAI says can grow in a basement, a bathroom, under the sink and anywhere with running water. [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/hay-fever/symptoms-causes/syc-20373039) singles out pet dander, which can bother people all year but may cause worse symptoms in winter when houses are closed up.

The fixes are indoor ones: mite-proof covers and weekly hot water washes for bedding, fixing leaks, and a dehumidifier where humidity is a problem, the ACAAI says. Our guides to [dust mite allergy](/blog/dust-mite-allergy) and [pet allergy treatment](/learn-about-ilit/pet-cat-dog-allergy-treatment), and our room by room [cleaning guide](/blog/how-to-clean-house-for-allergies), go further.

## Why allergy seasons are getting longer

Pollen seasons in North America have stretched out over the last few decades. A [2021 study in PNAS](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33558232/) used pollen counts from 60 stations between 1990 and 2018 and found seasons that started about 20 days earlier and ran about 8 days longer, with pollen concentrations up 21%. The authors tied the trend closely to warming and estimated that human-caused climate change accounted for about half of the trend in pollen seasons. [MedlinePlus](https://magazine.medlineplus.gov/article/what-triggers-seasonal-allergies) adds that rising carbon dioxide may make pollen more potent.

## Seasonal allergies symptoms

Seasonal allergy symptoms center on the nose and eyes. [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/hay-fever/symptoms-causes/syc-20373039) lists:

- a runny or stuffy nose
- watery, itchy, red eyes
- sneezing and cough
- an itchy nose, roof of the mouth or throat
- postnasal drip
- swollen, bruised looking skin under the eyes, called allergic shiners
- extreme tiredness, often from poor sleep

The [ACAAI](https://acaai.org/allergies/allergic-conditions/hay-fever/) puts a stuffy nose first when it ranks symptoms by how often they happen, and it says some people also get itchy skin or puffy eyelids.

Timing can help you tell an allergy from a cold. Mayo Clinic says hay fever starts right after exposure to the allergen and lasts as long as the exposure does, while a cold starts a day to three days after you catch the virus, can bring a low fever and body aches, and lasts three to seven days. Our [allergies or a cold](/blog/allergies-or-a-cold) article has a full symptom chart. If tightness in your chest comes with the season, our article on [allergies and chest tightness](/blog/can-allergies-cause-chest-tightness) explains what it can mean.

## How to ease seasonal allergies at home

These steps come from the AAAAI, the ACAAI and MedlinePlus:

1. **Check the pollen count.** [MedlinePlus](https://magazine.medlineplus.gov/article/what-triggers-seasonal-allergies) suggests following local counts from the National Allergy Bureau or a weather site and staying inside more when they're high. Our guide to [reading a pollen count](/blog/how-to-read-a-pollen-count) explains what the numbers mean.
2. **Keep the windows closed.** The [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/outdoor-allergens-ttr) says to use air conditioning if you can, since it cleans, cools and dries the air, and to keep car windows up too. The [ACAAI](https://acaai.org/allergies/allergic-conditions/hay-fever/) says to skip window fans, which can draw pollen and mold inside.
3. **Rinse off after being outside.** The AAAAI suggests a shower, a shampoo and a change of clothes when you come in.
4. **Hand off the mowing and raking.** Both stir up pollen and mold, the AAAAI says. If the lawn is yours to mow, MedlinePlus suggests a mask (an N95 gives the most protection) and eye protection.
5. **Dry laundry indoors.** Pollen may cling to sheets and towels hung outside, according to the ACAAI.
6. **Wear sunglasses.** Glasses or sunglasses help keep pollen out of your eyes, the ACAAI says, and rubbing your eyes irritates them and could make symptoms worse.

## Seasonal allergy medicine

Allergy medicine treats the symptoms while the season runs. The [ACAAI](https://acaai.org/allergies/allergic-conditions/hay-fever/) calls steroid nasal sprays (intranasal corticosteroids) the single most effective class of drug for allergic rhinitis, easing congestion, sneezing, itching and a runny nose. Antihistamines are also commonly used, sold as tablets, syrups, eye drops and nasal sprays. [MedlinePlus](https://magazine.medlineplus.gov/article/what-triggers-seasonal-allergies) adds saline rinses for congestion and says decongestants, which some antihistamines include, aren't for everyone.

An allergist quoted by the ACAAI says most over-the-counter medicines are designed for milder allergies and rarely do enough for moderate to severe ones. The ACAAI also notes that some people find an antihistamine becomes less effective as the allergy season worsens.

The [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/outdoor-allergens-ttr) says most hay fever medicines work best if you start them before a pollen season begins, and MedlinePlus says checking pollen counts can help you time the first dose.

## When to see an allergist

[Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/hay-fever/symptoms-causes/syc-20373039) suggests seeing a health care professional when you can't find relief, when allergy medicines don't help or cause side effects, or when you have another condition that makes hay fever worse, such as asthma, nasal polyps or frequent sinus infections. It also notes that many people, especially children, get so used to the symptoms that they might not seek treatment until they're severe.

[MedlinePlus](https://magazine.medlineplus.gov/article/what-triggers-seasonal-allergies) says a skin test or an allergen specific blood test can often help confirm which pollen you react to, and the AAAAI says knowing your triggers forms the basis of a treatment plan. Our comparison of [allergy skin tests and blood tests](/blog/allergy-skin-test-vs-blood-test) explains both.

## Longer term options for seasonal allergies

Medicine eases symptoms one season at a time. Immunotherapy goes after the allergy itself, and there are three forms.

**Allergy shots.** The [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/outdoor-allergens-ttr) says an allergist may recommend allergy shots if your symptoms continue or last many months of the year, and that shots can often provide long-term relief. [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/tests-procedures/allergy-shots/about/pac-20392876) describes a build-up phase of three to six months with shots one to three times a week, then a shot about once a month for three to five years or longer. Symptoms usually improve in the first year, with the biggest change often in the second. Our [full guide to allergy shots](/learn-about-ilit/allergy-shots-and-allergy-immunotherapy) explains how they work.

**Allergy tablets.** With sublingual immunotherapy, you dissolve a tablet under your tongue daily, starting several months before allergy season, the [ACAAI](https://acaai.org/allergies/allergic-conditions/hay-fever/) says. Only a few allergens can be treated this way: certain grass and ragweed pollens and house dust mite. The [FDA's list](https://www.fda.gov/vaccines-blood-biologics/allergenics/allergen-extract-sublingual-tablets) of these tablets has four: Grastek, Oralair, Ragwitek and Odactra.

## ILIT allergy treatment for seasonal allergies

ILIT allergy treatment puts small doses of FDA approved allergen extract into a lymph node, usually in the groin (just below the waist, near the hip), guided by ultrasound. Using a lymph node is an off label route, which our [ILIT FAQ](/learn-about-ilit#faq-fda) explains. A course is typically three injections over about two months, which makes it a fast allergy treatment course compared with years of shots, though relief isn't instant.

Grass pollen, a main trigger of late spring and summer allergies, was the focus of an early trial. In a [2008 trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19001265/) of 165 people with grass pollen hay fever, three lymph node allergy shots over two months gave tolerance three years later about equal to 54 standard shots over three years, with fewer side effects. The trial's corresponding author is named as the inventor on a patent for the method, and the research on ILIT is still smaller than the research on allergy shots.
