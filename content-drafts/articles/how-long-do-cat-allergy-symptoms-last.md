KEY TAKEAWAYS
- Cat allergy symptoms can come in two waves: within minutes of exposure, then again 4 to 8 hours later (Skoner, J Allergy Clin Immunol 2001).
- Cat allergen can keep causing symptoms for up to 6 months after a cat leaves a home (AAFA).
- In a study of 15 homes, about half took 20 to 24 weeks after the cat left for allergen levels to fall to those of cat free homes (Wood et al. 1989).
- Cat allergen turned up in 99.9% of U.S. homes in a national survey, though only about half had a cat (Arbes et al. 2004).

FAQS
Q: Are there hypoallergenic cats?
A: Not in the way the word suggests. The [ACAAI](https://acaai.org/allergies/allergic-conditions/pet-allergies/) says studies haven't shown that any cat is hypoallergenic, and that hair length and gender aren't linked to how much allergen a cat leaves around the house. What does make a difference is the number of cats: a home with more than one has higher allergen levels.

Q: Does bathing a cat help with cat allergies?
A: It may, for a short while. The [AAFA](https://aafa.org/allergies/types-of-allergies/pet-dog-cat-allergies/) suggests washing your pet every week and says it may cut the allergen in the air. Plenty of cats object, so if bathing isn't realistic, a HEPA air cleaner in the bedroom is the easier place to start.

Q: Why do I react to cat allergen in a home with no cat?
A: Because people carry it in. In a [national survey of 831 U.S. homes](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15241352/), cat allergen was found in 99.9% of them, even though a cat had lived in only about half during the prior six months. The researchers pointed to allergen carried on clothing as a likely reason. Offices, classrooms and friends' cars can all hold enough to set off a sensitive person.

Q: Is there a fast allergy treatment for cat allergy?
A: For relief in the moment, allergy medicine is the fastest option. Immunotherapy takes longer because it retrains how your immune system responds. Of the immunotherapy choices, ILIT has the shortest course, about three injections over about two months, though the research on ILIT for cat allergy specifically is small and its results are mixed. An allergist who offers it can tell you what they've seen in their own cat allergic patients.

Cat allergy symptoms usually start within minutes of exposure and can return 4 to 8 hours later, so a visit to a friend with a cat can still have you stuffed up that night. Once you're away from the allergen for good, most people feel better within a day or so. The catch is that cat allergen doesn't leave when the cat does. It can stay in a home for months, and as long as you're breathing it, symptoms can keep going.

The [Asthma and Allergy Foundation of America](https://aafa.org/allergies/types-of-allergies/pet-dog-cat-allergies/) (AAFA) says pet allergens can cause symptoms up to 6 months after the animal is gone, and that cat allergens are the worst for this.

## When to get care right away

Cat allergen is a common asthma trigger. The AAFA notes that an asthma episode can begin within 15 to 30 minutes of breathing in an allergen. If you have chest tightness, wheezing or trouble breathing around a cat and your rescue inhaler isn't helping, get emergency care. Throat swelling or trouble swallowing calls for 911.

## Why cat allergy symptoms come back hours later

Allergic reactions often arrive in two waves. A [review in the Journal of Allergy and Clinical Immunology](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11449200/) describes an early phase within minutes of exposure, with sneezing, itching and a clear runny nose. Then comes a late phase, 4 to 8 hours later, which brings congestion, tiredness and a general run down feeling.

That second wave fools people. You leave the party feeling fine, then wake up congested and assume you caught a cold.

How fast the first wave hits depends on how sensitive you are. Highly sensitive people can react within minutes of touching a cat, according to the [American College of Allergy, Asthma and Immunology](https://acaai.org/allergies/allergic-conditions/pet-allergies/) (ACAAI). At the other end, the AAFA says that when allergen levels are low, symptoms may not show up until after several days of contact.

## Why cat allergen hangs around so long

The problem isn't fur. Cat allergens come from the skin and saliva, the ACAAI says, and they spread through the house on dander and loose hair. The main one is a protein called Fel d 1.

It's sticky, and some of it is small enough to float. In a [study of British homes](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9577519/), about 23% of the airborne Fel d 1 was on small particles. Allergist Dana Wallace, MD, quoted by the ACAAI, says the allergen stays in the air for at least 30 minutes after something stirs it up. Sitting down on a couch can be enough.

Then it settles into carpet, upholstery and bedding. When researchers at Johns Hopkins [tracked 15 homes after the cat was removed](https://doi.org/10.1016/0091-6749(89)90006-7), allergen levels fell slowly. About half the homes reached the levels of a cat free home after 20 to 24 weeks. Others took longer.

So if you move into a place where a cat used to live, expect to notice it for a while. An [AAFA allergist answer](https://community.aafa.org/db/ask-the-allergist/record/can-a-home-that-used-to-have-a-cat-trigger-my-asthma-or-allergies) from Matthew Greenhawt, MD, says levels can stay high for several months but don't pose a long term risk after that.

## What helps in the first few hours

Wash your hands with soap and water after touching a cat, as the ACAAI recommends. Since cat allergen travels on clothing, changing your clothes after a long visit makes sense too.

Medicine does most of the work. The ACAAI lists oral antihistamines and steroid nasal sprays for nose symptoms, antihistamine eye drops for itchy eyes, and inhaled medicines for chest symptoms. If you know a visit is coming, ask your doctor or pharmacist about timing, because some of these work better when they're taken ahead.

## If you live with a cat

Most allergic cat owners keep their cats, and the goal is lowering the dose rather than reaching zero. A few steps have decent support:

- **Keep the cat out of the bedroom.** You spend a third of your life there. The ACAAI notes that shutting a cat in one room won't keep the allergen in that room, so the bedroom is the room to protect.
- **Run a HEPA air cleaner.** In the British homes study, a HEPA air cleaner significantly cut airborne cat allergen. The ACAAI suggests running one continuously in the bedroom or living room.
- **Vacuum weekly with a HEPA vacuum**, as the AAFA recommends for carpets and fabric furniture.

These help. They rarely solve it, which is why many people with a cat at home end up looking at treatment that changes the allergy itself.

## Longer term options for cat allergy

Allergy medicine controls symptoms while you take it. It doesn't change how your immune system reacts to cats. Immunotherapy does, by exposing your body to small, controlled amounts of cat allergen until it stops overreacting.

**Allergy shots (SCIT)** are the standard option, and the ACAAI and AAFA both list them for cat allergy. The [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/allergy-shots-%28immunotherapy%29) describes a build-up phase of shots once or twice a week for four to six months, then maintenance shots every two to four weeks for three to five years. [How Often Do You Get Allergy Shots?](/blog/how-often-do-you-get-allergy-shots) adds up the visits.

**Allergy tablets (SLIT)** aren't an option for cats in the U.S. yet. The [FDA's list of allergy tablets](https://www.fda.gov/vaccines-blood-biologics/allergenics/allergen-extract-sublingual-tablets) has four products, and none is for cat allergy.

**ILIT (intralymphatic immunotherapy)** is the shortest course, and for some people a faster alternative to allergy shots. An allergist injects FDA approved allergen extract into a lymph node, usually near the groin, guided by ultrasound. Using the extract this way is off label. A typical course is three injections over two months, spread 4 weeks apart, based on the schedule used in the [2008 trial that established ILIT](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2582048/), which studied grass pollen.

For cat allergy, the evidence is early and mixed. A [2012 trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22464647/) found a large gain in nasal tolerance after three injections, but it used an engineered cat protein made by the company that funded the study, not the standard extract. A [2021 placebo controlled trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8178859/) in Korea, which included cat allergic patients, found people used less medicine at four months, yet their symptom scores were no better than placebo, and some had wheezing or shortness of breath after injections. If a practice offers ILIT for cat allergy, ask how many cat patients they've treated and how those patients did a year later. That answer is more useful than any general claim.

The research on ILIT is promising but smaller than for allergy shots, and results vary from person to person. Our [comparison of allergy shots, allergy drops and ILIT](/learn-about-ilit/allergy-shots-vs-allergy-drops-vs-ilit) puts the three side by side, and the [Pet Allergy Treatment guide](/learn-about-ilit/pet-cat-dog-allergy-treatment) covers cats and dogs together. An allergist can confirm which allergens you react to with a [skin or blood test](/blog/allergy-skin-test-vs-blood-test), which matters because immunotherapy is matched to your specific allergens.
