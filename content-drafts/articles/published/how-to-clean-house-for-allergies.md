KEY TAKEAWAYS
- If you have allergies, have someone else clean while you're out, or wear an N95 mask and goggles. With severe allergies, stay out of a vacuumed room for about two hours.
- Dust with a damp cloth and vacuum weekly with a HEPA vacuum. A dry cloth can send dust back into the air.
- Clean mold off hard surfaces with detergent and water, then fix the leak or damp that let it grow, or it will most likely come back.
- Use a MERV 13 furnace filter if your system can take one, and keep indoor humidity between 30% and 50%.
- Cleaning lowers exposure. If symptoms keep going anyway, an allergy test shows what to target and opens the door to immunotherapy.

FAQS
Q: Why are my allergies worse after cleaning?
A: Cleaning stirs settled allergen back into the air. The [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/should-you-have-air-ducts-your-home-cleaned) notes that vacuuming can raise the amount of dust in the air both during and after the job, and the [AAFA](https://aafa.org/allergies/types-of-allergies/pet-dog-cat-allergies/) says pet allergen can stay airborne for a long time once it's disturbed. Strong cleaning sprays can add irritation of their own. Damp dusting keeps more of it down, and a mask helps protect you while you work.

Q: How often should you vacuum if you have allergies?
A: Once a week is the usual advice. [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/allergies/in-depth/allergy/art-20049365) says to vacuum carpet weekly, and the [AAFA](https://community.aafa.org/blog/spring-cleaning-for-an-asthma-and-allergy-friendly-home) says to vacuum mattresses, fabric furniture and wall to wall carpet weekly. With a furry pet at home, the [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/indoor-allergens-ttr) says to vacuum carpets often or swap them for hard flooring.

Q: Should you wear a mask when cleaning?
A: If you have allergies or asthma and nobody else can do the cleaning, yes. The [AAFA](https://community.aafa.org/blog/spring-cleaning-for-an-asthma-and-allergy-friendly-home) suggests an N95 mask and goggles, with your hair covered, because vacuuming, dusting and mold cleanup send allergens toward your eyes, nose and lungs. For mold, the [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home) adds long gloves and goggles without vent holes, and says the respirator has to fit properly.

Q: Is carpet or hardwood better for allergies?
A: Hard floors. The [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/managing-indoor-allergen) recommends removing wall to wall carpet as much as possible and using throw rugs you clean regularly instead. If replacing carpet isn't an option, [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/allergies/in-depth/allergy/art-20049365) suggests low pile carpet, vacuumed weekly with a HEPA vacuum and shampooed often.

Q: What air filter is best for allergies?
A: For a central heating and cooling system, the [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/guide-air-cleaners-home) recommends a filter rated at least MERV 13, or the highest rating your system can take. Most systems handle MERV 13 fine as long as the filter is changed often, and an HVAC technician can confirm yours. For a single room, a portable air cleaner with a HEPA filter does the same job on a smaller scale.

Q: Does an air purifier help with allergies?
A: It can help some. The [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/guide-air-cleaners-home) says several studies of portable HEPA air cleaners found improvement in one or more allergy or asthma symptoms, though the gains weren't always large and didn't cover every symptom. Pick one rated for your room's size, put it where you spend the most time, and skip any model that makes ozone, a lung irritant.

Q: Does duct cleaning help allergies?
A: There's no good evidence that it does. The [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/should-you-have-air-ducts-your-home-cleaned) says duct cleaning has never been shown to prevent health problems and doesn't recommend it as routine maintenance. It suggests considering it only when there's substantial visible mold inside the ducts, a pest infestation, or so much debris that dust blows out of the vents.

Q: Can cleaning products cause allergies?
A: They can make you feel worse. The [American Lung Association](https://www.lung.org/clean-air/indoor-air/indoor-air-pollutants/cleaning-supplies-household-chem) says chemicals released by many cleaning products, called volatile organic compounds (VOCs), contribute to allergic reactions and breathing problems, and the [ACAAI](https://acaai.org/resource/home-allergy-management) counts aerosols among the household triggers that cause allergy-like symptoms without an immune reaction. Warm water and soap often do the trick, the American Lung Association adds.

Q: Is there a fast allergy treatment for indoor allergies?
A: Allergy medicine gives the quickest relief, but it works only while you keep taking it. For dust mites, pet dander and mold, immunotherapy treats the allergy itself. The [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/allergy-shots-%28immunotherapy%29) describes allergy shots continuing for three to five years once you reach maintenance, while ILIT (intralymphatic immunotherapy) is typically about three injections over about two months, as in the [2008 trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2582048/) that set its schedule, with a smaller body of research behind it. To ask an allergist about it, use [Find a Provider](/find-an-ilit-provider) on OpenAirAllergyNetwork.com to locate an allergist who offers it.

When you clean your house for allergies, how you clean matters as much as how often. Dusting and vacuuming can stir allergens back into the air, the [AAFA](https://community.aafa.org/blog/spring-cleaning-for-an-asthma-and-allergy-friendly-home) warns, and [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/dust-mites/diagnosis-treatment/drc-20352178) says a damp or oiled cloth or mop keeps dust from going into the air the way a dry one can. The [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/indoor-allergens-ttr) calls avoiding allergens the most effective treatment approach, and the right cleaning routine is how to reduce allergens in your home, especially if someone else does the dusting and vacuuming while you're out.

Strict avoidance isn't always possible, the [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/indoor-allergens-ttr) notes, so the goal is less allergen where you spend the most time, mainly the bedroom and the rooms where you sit. Below are the supplies and the steps for each room, starting with who should do the cleaning. If symptoms keep coming back after all that, immunotherapy treats the allergy itself, and the last section covers allergy shots, allergy drops and ILIT (intralymphatic immunotherapy), for some people a faster alternative to allergy shots.

## When to get care right away

Cleaning stirs allergens into the air, where they can reach your lungs, the [AAFA](https://community.aafa.org/blog/spring-cleaning-for-an-asthma-and-allergy-friendly-home) warns, and the [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/indoor-allergens-ttr) notes indoor allergens can set off asthma flare-ups in people with allergic asthma. If you have trouble breathing while cleaning or afterward, or your rescue inhaler isn't helping, call 911 or get emergency care.

## What you'll need for house cleaning for allergies

- A vacuum with a HEPA filter or a double layer bag
- Microfiber cloths and a mop you can use damp
- An N95 mask and goggles, if you'll be cleaning yourself
- Zippered allergen proof covers for the mattress and pillows
- A hygrometer (humidity meter) from the hardware store

## Before you start: who cleans, and how

Vacuuming, dusting and mold cleanup are the jobs most likely to throw allergens into your eyes, nose and lungs, according to the [AAFA](https://community.aafa.org/blog/spring-cleaning-for-an-asthma-and-allergy-friendly-home). It suggests having someone else clean while you're out of the room. If that's not possible, wear an N95 mask and goggles and cover your hair.

[Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/dust-mites/diagnosis-treatment/drc-20352178) says people with severe allergies should stay out of a freshly vacuumed room for about two hours. It also recommends a damp or oiled cloth or mop over a dry one, which keeps dust from going into the air and coming back down.

## How to clean the bedroom for allergies

You spend about a third of your life in the bedroom, the [ACAAI](https://acaai.org/resource/home-allergy-management) points out, so it comes first.

1. **Cover and wash the bed.** Zippered covers go on the mattress, box spring and pillows, and bedding gets a weekly wash in water of at least 130°F, says the [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/indoor-allergens-ttr). Our dust mite guide has the fallback if your hot water doesn't get that hot.
2. **Dust high and behind.** The [AAFA's checklist](https://community.aafa.org/blog/spring-cleaning-for-an-asthma-and-allergy-friendly-home) includes ceiling fans, light fixtures, closet shelves, and the dust on and behind dressers and nightstands. It also says to replace pillows older than two years.
3. **Cut the clutter.** Knickknacks, books and magazines collect dust, [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/allergies/in-depth/allergy/art-20049365) says, and toys and stuffed animals can go in plastic bins.
4. **Swap fabrics you can't wash.** Mayo Clinic suggests washable cotton or synthetic curtains and roller shades in place of horizontal blinds.
5. **Keep pets out.** Even if a pet stays in the home, Mayo Clinic says to keep it out of the bedroom.

## How to clean floors and furniture

1. **Vacuum weekly.** Mayo Clinic recommends a weekly pass over carpet with a HEPA vacuum, and the [AAFA](https://aafa.org/allergies/types-of-allergies/pet-dog-cat-allergies/) says to vacuum carpet and fabric furniture weekly with one. The AAFA's cleaning checklist adds mattresses. Vacuuming lifts surface dust but leaves most dust mites behind, Mayo's [dust mite page](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/dust-mites/diagnosis-treatment/drc-20352178) notes, so it works alongside the bedroom steps.
2. **Damp-mop hard floors.** The AAFA says to mop under furniture too.
3. **Choose hard floors when you can.** The [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/managing-indoor-allergen) recommends removing wall to wall carpet and using throw rugs you clean regularly. Renting, or not ready to rip it out? Mayo Clinic suggests low pile carpet, vacuumed weekly and shampooed often.
4. **Wash throw rugs and curtains weekly** in water at 130°F or hotter, per the AAFA.

## How to clean the kitchen for allergies

To keep cockroaches out of the kitchen, the [AAFA](https://aafa.org/allergies/types-of-allergies/insect-allergy/cockroach-allergy/) says to store food in airtight containers, wash dishes instead of leaving them out, wipe up crumbs and spills, and fix leaky pipes, since cockroaches like damp places. It also says baits and traps may be better choices than sprays, which may irritate allergies and asthma in some people.

For mold, [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/allergies/in-depth/allergy/art-20049365) points to the refrigerator: wipe up moisture, empty and clean the drip pan, and clean or replace moldy door seals. Use a stove exhaust fan that vents outside to remove cooking moisture.

## How to clean the bathroom and other damp spots for mold

Mold grows where there's moisture, and the [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home) says that if you clean up mold without fixing the water problem, it will most likely come back.

1. **Run the fan.** Use the bathroom exhaust fan or open a window when you shower, the EPA says. Mayo Clinic adds towel-drying the tub and enclosure afterward.
2. **Scrub with detergent and water.** That's the EPA's method for mold on hard surfaces, followed by drying completely. The EPA doesn't recommend bleach as a routine step, and it warns never to mix bleach with cleaners containing ammonia. The [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/indoor-allergens-ttr) adds 5% bleach only if necessary.
3. **Remove it, don't just kill it.** Dead mold can still cause allergic reactions in some people, according to the EPA.
4. **Dry leaks fast.** Wet areas dried within 24 to 48 hours usually won't grow mold, the EPA says. Moldy patches larger than about 10 square feet, roughly 3 feet by 3 feet, call for the EPA's larger cleanup guidance or an experienced professional. Renters should report leaks to the building owner right away.
5. **Keep humidity down.** The EPA's ideal is 30% to 50%, checked with an inexpensive humidity meter. An air conditioner or dehumidifier can lower it.

## How to clean house for pet allergies

Pet allergen clings to walls, carpet and fabric furniture, the [AAFA](https://aafa.org/allergies/types-of-allergies/pet-dog-cat-allergies/) says. It can stay at high levels for months, and can cause symptoms up to six months after the animal is gone, especially with cats.

1. **Hand off the grooming.** The AAFA suggests someone without a pet allergy brush the pet outside and clean the litter box or cage. If it has to be you, wear a mask and gloves. For rabbits, hamsters, guinea pigs and mice, the allergen is in their urine, so the [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/indoor-allergens-ttr) also recommends a non-allergic family member clean the cage.
2. **Bathe the pet weekly.** It may reduce the allergen in the dander they shed, according to [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/allergies/in-depth/allergy/art-20049365).
3. **Cover the couch.** The AAFA suggests slipcovers or blankets on fabric furniture, washed weekly in hot water.

No dog or cat breed is allergy free, the AAAAI says. Our guide to [dog allergy](/blog/allergic-to-my-dog) covers living with a dog.

## Air filters and air purifiers for allergies

The [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/guide-air-cleaners-home) recommends a furnace or HVAC filter rated at least MERV 13, or the highest your system can take. Change it as the maker recommends, typically every 60 to 90 days. A central filter only cleans air while the system is running, the EPA notes.

For one room, a portable air cleaner with a HEPA filter fits the job. Choose one whose clean air delivery rate (CADR) matches the room's size and put it where you spend the most time. Avoid any model that produces ozone, which irritates the lungs.

Air duct cleaning has never been shown to prevent health problems, the [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/should-you-have-air-ducts-your-home-cleaned) says, and it suggests considering duct cleaning only for substantial visible mold, pests or heavy debris inside the ducts.

## How to keep pollen out of your house

During pollen season, the [AAFA](https://aafa.org/allergies/types-of-allergies/pollen-allergy/) suggests keeping windows closed, taking off your shoes at the door, and showering before bed so pollen stays off your bedding. It also says to wash clothes worn outdoors and dry laundry inside instead of on a line.

## Cleaning products and allergies

Chemicals given off by many cleaning products contribute to allergic reactions and breathing problems, says the [American Lung Association](https://www.lung.org/clean-air/indoor-air/indoor-air-pollutants/cleaning-supplies-household-chem). It says warm water and soap often do the trick, and it recommends keeping the room well ventilated while you clean and skipping air fresheners. A "green" label doesn't guarantee a product is safer.

## Longer term options when cleaning isn't enough

Even a spotless home holds some allergen, and strict avoidance isn't always possible, the [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/indoor-allergens-ttr) says. An allergist can test you to find which indoor allergen is behind your symptoms. That tells you which of the steps above matter most, and our guide to [allergy skin tests vs. blood tests](/blog/allergy-skin-test-vs-blood-test) explains how testing works.

Allergy medicine eases symptoms while you take it. To treat the allergy itself, immunotherapy trains your immune system to tolerate dust mites, pet dander, mold or pollen. Allergy shots have the longest track record: the [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/allergy-shots-%28immunotherapy%29) describes weekly or twice weekly shots for four to six months, then maintenance shots every two to four weeks for three to five years. Allergy drops and tablets (sublingual immunotherapy, or SLIT) go under the tongue and are taken at home, the [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/aeroallergen-immunotherapy) explains. SLIT tablets are FDA approved for grass pollen, ragweed and dust mite, including the dust mite tablet [ODACTRA](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=71ca220d-78d5-47d4-be84-8d6e844b24b3), whose first dose is given at a doctor's office. Drops are an off label use of approved extracts.

ILIT is a much shorter course. An allergist injects FDA approved allergen extract into a lymph node at the top of the thigh, near the hip, guided by ultrasound, an off label use of the extract. A course is typically about three injections over about two months, the schedule from the [2008 trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2582048/) that established it. Its research is promising but smaller than the research on allergy shots, as our [Learn About ILIT](/learn-about-ilit) page explains. Our [comparison of allergy shots, allergy drops and ILIT](/learn-about-ilit/allergy-shots-vs-allergy-drops-vs-ilit) puts the options side by side.

To talk with an allergist about a shorter course, [find an ILIT provider near you](/find-an-ilit-provider).
