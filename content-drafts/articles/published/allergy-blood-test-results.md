KEY TAKEAWAYS
- Allergy blood test results are reported in kilounits per liter (kU/L), on a scale of about 0.1 to 100.
- Labs don't share one class scale: a 3.50 kU/L result is class 2 at one national lab and class 3 at two others.
- A higher number or class means your body is more likely to recognize the allergen, but it doesn't show how severe the allergy is.
- About 50% to 60% of positive allergy blood tests don't turn out to be an allergy.
- Before allergy shots or other immunotherapy, an allergist will want to retest you to confirm your allergens.

FAQS
Q: How accurate are allergy blood tests?
A: Allergy blood tests rarely miss an allergy, but many of their positive results turn out not to be one. [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/treatments/22345-allergy-blood-test) says about 50% to 60% of positive allergy blood tests don't turn out to be an allergy, while [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/lab-tests/allergy-blood-test/) calls false negatives uncommon. That's why an allergist reads the results alongside your symptoms and history, and the [ACAAI](https://acaai.org/allergies/testing-diagnosis/) says skin testing is the method allergists prefer and usually the most accurate.

Q: How long do allergy blood test results take?
A: Usually a few days. [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/treatments/22345-allergy-blood-test) says the wait can run a little longer or shorter, since the sample goes to a lab, and then the provider who ordered the test goes over the results with you. An allergy skin test is faster: the [AAFA](https://aafa.org/allergies/allergy-diagnosis/) says it's done in the office with results in 15 to 20 minutes.

Q: Can a positive allergy blood test lead to allergy shots?
A: It can, when the positive allergens match your symptoms and an allergist confirms them. The [ACAAI](https://acaai.org/resource/allergy-testing/) says an allergist who recommends immunotherapy will want to retest you to confirm your allergens first. Allergy shots contain a tiny but increasing amount of the allergen you're sensitive to, the ACAAI says, and the [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/allergy-shots-%28immunotherapy%29) says maintenance shots typically continue for three to five years. ILIT (intralymphatic immunotherapy) is typically three injections over about two months. To ask whether your results fit it, use [Find a Provider](/find-an-ilit-provider) on OpenAirAllergyNetwork.com to locate an allergist who offers it.

Q: What does a class 2 allergy mean?
A: Class 2 is a middle level of IgE to that allergen, and ARUP and Labcorp both label it moderate, but labs draw it at different numbers: 0.71 to 3.50 kU/L on [ARUP Laboratories'](https://www.aruplab.com/Testing-Information/resources/HotLines/Sample_Reports/Aug2019/3001729_Allergen%20Region%2019%20Respiratory%20Panel%20IgE_REG19PANEL.pdf) scale, 0.56 to 1.40 on [Labcorp's](https://www.labcorp.com/content/dam/labcorp/files/allergy-launch/225004-0822_DX_QRG_Allergy%20Report_Final.pdf), and 0.70 to 3.49 at [Michigan Medicine's lab](https://mlabs.umich.edu/tests/cherry-ige), which calls it positive. Check the class key printed on your own report, or the chart comparing three labs in our [allergy blood test results guide](/blog/allergy-blood-test-results). The [AAFA](https://aafa.org/allergies/allergy-diagnosis/) says the class doesn't reflect how severe an allergy is. If a class 2 allergen lines up with symptoms you get around it, take the result to an allergist to talk about treatment. If it doesn't, it may mean sensitization without an allergy.

Q: What is the difference between total IgE and specific IgE?
A: Total IgE is the overall amount of IgE antibody in your blood, and specific IgE is measured one allergen at a time. [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/lab-tests/allergy-blood-test/) says a high total can mean you have some kind of allergy but doesn't show what you're allergic to. [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/treatments/22345-allergy-blood-test) calls the specific test the more sensitive of the two, so the numbers listed for each allergen are the ones that point to what you may be allergic to.

Q: Can you have high IgE but no allergies?
A: Yes. A high total IgE on its own doesn't show what you're allergic to, and [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/lab-tests/allergy-blood-test/) says smoking or a parasitic infection can also raise it. The [ACAAI](https://acaai.org/allergies/testing-diagnosis/) says blood and skin test results alone don't diagnose allergies and have to be read together with your medical history, so ask the provider who ordered the test what else could explain a high number.

Q: Can you have allergies with normal IgE?
A: Yes, especially if only total IgE was measured. [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/treatments/22345-allergy-blood-test) says many people with subtle allergies may have a normal total IgE level. In its [interpretation guide](https://corporate.thermofisher.com/content/dam/phadia/library/en/Quick_guide_to_using_ImmunoCAP_Specific_IgE_testing.pdf), Thermo Fisher, which makes the ImmunoCAP test, says about 30% of patients show raised IgE to a specific allergen with a normal total, which is why it doesn't recommend screening with total IgE. If your symptoms point to an allergy, ask whether specific IgE was tested for the allergens you suspect, or whether an allergy skin test makes sense.

Q: What does 0.35 kU/L mean on an allergy test?
A: kU/L stands for kilounits per liter, the unit labs use for the amount of IgE antibody to an allergen in your blood, and results run on a scale of about 0.1 to 100, the [AAFA](https://aafa.org/allergies/allergy-diagnosis/) says. On a [sample report from ARUP Laboratories](https://www.aruplab.com/Testing-Information/resources/HotLines/Sample_Reports/Aug2019/3001729_Allergen%20Region%2019%20Respiratory%20Panel%20IgE_REG19PANEL.pdf), 0.35 is the bottom of class 1, labeled low, and it's flagged as just above the normal range of 0.34 or less. [Labcorp](https://www.labcorp.com/content/dam/labcorp/files/allergy-launch/225004-0822_DX_QRG_Allergy%20Report_Final.pdf) also calls it class I, low, while [Michigan Medicine's lab](https://mlabs.umich.edu/tests/cherry-ige) calls 0.35 to 0.69 equivocal. A low result like this matters only if you have symptoms around that allergen, so read it against your own lab's range and your symptoms.

An allergist needs to know which allergens are behind your symptoms before recommending treatment, and an allergy skin test or blood test is how they usually find out. When your allergy blood test results come back, each allergen you were tested for gets a number in kilounits per liter (kU/L), and most labs add a class from 0 to 6. A higher number means your body is more likely to recognize that allergen, not that the allergy is worse, according to the [Asthma and Allergy Foundation of America](https://aafa.org/allergies/allergy-diagnosis/) (AAFA). A positive result counts as an allergy only when it matches the symptoms you get around that allergen.

An allergy blood test looks for the same allergens a skin test does. The [American College of Allergy, Asthma and Immunology](https://acaai.org/allergies/testing-diagnosis/) (ACAAI) says both can detect sensitivity to inhalants like pollen and dust mites, and to medicines, certain foods, latex and insect venom. [Cleveland Clinic's](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/treatments/22345-allergy-blood-test) list for the blood test also includes molds and pet dander. These IgE allergy tests aren't the IgG "food sensitivity" tests sold online, which the AAFA and the [American Academy of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/allergy-testing) (AAAAI) don't recommend.

The ACAAI says allergists choose tests based on your symptoms, exposures, age and even hobbies, then interpret every result in the context of your medical history, so your results are one piece of the picture. That judgment decides treatment too: allergy shots, allergy drops and ILIT (intralymphatic immunotherapy), which some people choose as a faster alternative to allergy shots, all target the specific allergens an allergist confirms.

## How an allergy blood test works, step by step

1. **The order.** Your health care provider may order the test if you have allergy symptoms, [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/lab-tests/allergy-blood-test/) says.
2. **The blood draw.** A health care professional takes a sample from a vein in your arm, which usually takes less than five minutes. The AAFA says your doctor or a lab draws it and sends it out for analysis.
3. **The wait.** Cleveland Clinic says results usually take a few days, though it can be more or less.
4. **The results review.** Your provider compares the numbers with your symptoms and history, which the ACAAI says is how every result should be read.

A skin test takes one office visit: the AAFA says results come in 15 to 20 minutes, and the ACAAI says blood tests cost more than skin tests. A blood test is generally used when skin testing might be unsafe or won't work, according to the AAAAI, and the AAFA names two common reasons: a skin condition, or medicines that interfere with skin testing.

The ACAAI says medicine doesn't interfere with blood test results, though MedlinePlus says you may be asked to stop antihistamines before the draw. Follow the instructions that come with your lab order.

## How to read allergy blood test results

A typical report lists each allergen tested, a number beside it and the lab's normal range.

1. **Check the units.** Allergen-specific IgE is reported in kU/L, sometimes written kUA/L. [Thermo Fisher](https://corporate.thermofisher.com/content/dam/phadia/library/en/Quick_guide_to_using_ImmunoCAP_Specific_IgE_testing.pdf), which makes the ImmunoCAP test, describes the unit as kilounits of allergen-specific IgE per liter of blood. Your lab order may call the test ImmunoCAP, CAP or RAST, names used by the AAFA and MedlinePlus.
2. **Compare each number with your lab's range.** On a [sample report from ARUP Laboratories](https://www.aruplab.com/Testing-Information/resources/HotLines/Sample_Reports/Aug2019/3001729_Allergen%20Region%2019%20Respiratory%20Panel%20IgE_REG19PANEL.pdf), a national reference lab, the normal range beside each allergen reads 0.34 kU/L or less, and a 0.35 is flagged high. Thermo Fisher counts 0.1 or more as a sign of sensitization, so the 0.10 to 0.34 band is a gray zone that ARUP leaves to specialists.
3. **Find the class.** Many reports turn each number into a class, and labs draw the class lines in different places, as the chart below shows.
4. **Find total IgE.** It sits on its own line with its own normal range, separate from the numbers for each allergen.
5. **Match each positive to your symptoms.** The ACAAI's [allergy testing Q&A](https://acaai.org/resource/allergy-testing/) says allergy tests confirm what your health history already tells the allergist. Its example: a positive dust mite test only matters if you sneeze or get itchy eyes when you vacuum or stir up house dust.

A dust mite result of 3.50 kU/L is class 2, moderate, at ARUP, class 3, high, at Labcorp and class 3, positive, at Michigan Medicine. Cleveland Clinic explains that labs use different brands of allergy blood tests, so scoring varies. Go by the class and range printed on your own report. Equivocal, a label Labcorp and Michigan Medicine use, means borderline. None of the three scales has separate class cutoffs for children and adults.

### Does "very high" mean a severe allergy?

No. Labels like high and very high describe how much IgE is in your blood, not how bad your reactions are. The AAFA says the score and class don't reflect how severe an allergy is, and that no skin or blood test can tell how severe it is. That includes skin tests: the AAFA says a positive skin test doesn't predict how severe a reaction will be, and a bigger bump doesn't always mean more symptoms. ARUP's report adds that higher levels may not match how strongly you react to the allergen.

What a higher number does tell your allergist is how likely the allergen is to cause a reaction. A [practical guide for clinicians in the Cleveland Clinic Journal of Medicine](https://www.ccjm.org/content/78/9/585) says the chance of an allergic reaction often rises with the level of specific IgE, though the level doesn't predict severity or guarantee a reaction. The same guide says classes 1 and 2 often go with a low likelihood of a reaction, while classes 5 and 6 generally go with reactions when you're exposed.

According to the AAFA, the only test that can show how severe an allergy may be is a challenge test. In the doctor's office, you breathe in or swallow a tiny amount of the allergen, and the dose slowly goes up while a physician, usually an allergist, watches for symptoms. The AAFA says a physician should supervise because of the risk of anaphylaxis, a severe allergic reaction. It's mostly used for possible food and medicine allergies.

## Positive allergy blood test: what it can and can't tell you

Blood test results alone don't diagnose an allergy. The [ACAAI](https://acaai.org/allergies/testing-diagnosis/) says blood and skin test results have to be read with your medical history, and when results are unclear or don't fit, allergists rely on the history and an exam to make the final call.

A positive allergy blood test means your immune system has made IgE to that allergen, but the AAFA says that doesn't necessarily mean the allergen caused your symptoms. Cleveland Clinic says about 50% to 60% of positive results don't turn out to be an allergy, which is why it says these tests should be ordered based on your signs and symptoms. The ACAAI agrees that allergy tests shouldn't be ordered at random.

Some positive results come years before symptoms do. In a [Danish study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12532111/) that followed 15 adults with a positive birch pollen test but no symptoms, 9 developed birch pollen allergy within three years, and a specific IgE of class 2 or higher was a strong predictor. A [larger follow-up](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21293149/) of 52 young people sensitized to grass or birch pollen found a lower risk, about 5% a year, and those who did develop symptoms had higher IgE levels. Both studies looked at pollen, not foods. If a result doesn't match your symptoms today, it's worth keeping the report and mentioning it if symptoms show up later.

Total IgE, the overall amount of IgE in your blood, tells you even less than the numbers for each allergen. Cleveland Clinic says it doesn't show what you're allergic to and that many people with subtle allergies may have a normal total. A high total can also come from smoking or a parasitic infection, according to MedlinePlus. Its normal range depends on age, Thermo Fisher says, and on the lab: the ARUP sample patient's range ran up to 214 kU/L, while [Michigan Medicine's lab](https://mlabs.umich.edu/tests/immunoglobulin-e-quantitation) lists 2 to 150 kU/L.

Before immunotherapy like allergy shots or ILIT, the ACAAI's Q&A says your allergist will want to retest you to confirm your allergens and that the treatment plan fits, and the ACAAI calls skin testing the method allergists prefer.

### Component testing for food allergies

A positive food result can lead to a second blood test that sorts out what it means. The ACAAI describes a peanut component test that shows which peanut proteins your IgE targets. Ara h 1, Ara h 2 and Ara h 3 carry more risk than others, and the results help an allergist decide whether to do an in-office food challenge. Cleveland Clinic notes that certain component tests can predict severity, which standard blood tests usually don't show.

In an oral food challenge, the [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/what-do-patients-and-caregivers-need-to-know-about) says, you eat the food slowly, in gradually increasing amounts, under medical supervision. It's usually done when your history and skin and blood tests are inconclusive. If you're at risk of anaphylaxis, MedlinePlus says you may need to carry an epinephrine auto-injector, so ask your provider whether that applies to you.

## Negative allergy blood test but still have symptoms?

MedlinePlus calls false negatives uncommon with allergy blood tests. Still, ARUP's report notes that a negative test may not rule out an allergy, so a negative with clear symptoms is worth taking back to your allergist.

Your allergist may order a skin test, which the ACAAI calls the preferred and usually most accurate method. Or the cause may not be allergic at all: the ACAAI's testing Q&A says people without an identifiable trigger could have a nonallergic nasal condition.

## Longer term options

Thermo Fisher's guide suggests ranking your results from highest to lowest, cutting exposure to the highest ones first and focusing on indoor allergens, since those may be easier to control. Allergy medicine eases symptoms day to day. Immunotherapy goes after the allergy itself, and the ACAAI says shots contain a tiny but increasing amount of the allergen you're sensitive to.

**Allergy shots (SCIT)** start with a build-up phase of shots once or twice a week for four to six months, then maintenance shots every two to four weeks for three to five years, according to the [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/allergy-shots-%28immunotherapy%29).

**Allergy drops or tablets (SLIT)** go under the tongue. The ACAAI says immunotherapy under the tongue, like shots, uses gradually increasing doses of your allergen.

**ILIT (intralymphatic immunotherapy)** is the shortest of the three courses, for people who want a fast allergy treatment schedule. An allergist injects FDA approved allergen extract into a lymph node in the groin (just below the waist, near the hip), an off label route, typically three times over about two months. Research is promising but smaller than for allergy shots, and results vary. The [Learn About ILIT](/learn-about-ilit) page covers who may be a candidate.

If you just got your blood test results, an allergist who offers ILIT can go over them with your history and tell you which allergens a short course could treat. [Find an ILIT allergy treatment provider near you](/find-an-ilit-provider) to start that conversation.
