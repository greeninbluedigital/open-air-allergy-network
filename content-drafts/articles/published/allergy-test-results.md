KEY TAKEAWAYS
- Read the two control spots first: histamine should raise a bump and saline shouldn't.
- European skin test standards count a wheal of 3 millimeters or more as positive.
- A positive skin test shows sensitization, which isn't the same as having an allergy.
- A bigger wheal makes a true allergy more likely, but it doesn't predict how severe a reaction will be.
- A "4+" doesn't mean exactly the same thing at every clinic, so ask which scale yours uses.

FAQS
Q: What does a positive allergy test look like?
A: A positive spot looks and feels like a bug bite. [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003519.htm) says positive skin tests turn red, itchy and swollen like a bug bite, and your provider will see a red, raised area called a wheal. The [AAFA](https://aafa.org/allergies/allergy-diagnosis/) describes a wheal as a raised, round area that looks like a hive, and says the test spot also gets red, swollen and itchy.

Q: What does wheal size mean on an allergy test?
A: Size tells your allergist how likely the allergen is to matter, not how bad a reaction will be. The [AAFA](https://aafa.org/allergies/allergy-diagnosis/) says a larger wheal usually means your body recognizes the allergen more, though not always that it causes more symptoms. A [European study of 3,068 patients](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4215109/) found the chance of symptoms rose with wheal size for 17 of 18 airborne allergens.

Q: Can you have a positive allergy test but no symptoms?
A: Yes. [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003519.htm) says people can test positive to a substance and have no problems with it in everyday life, and that food testing carries a high risk of these false positives. The [ACAAI](https://acaai.org/allergies/treatment/allergy-testing/skin-test) says the same: a positive result doesn't mean you'll react when you're exposed later.

Q: What does 4+ mean in allergy testing?
A: On both plus scales in one skin test device maker's [instructions for use](https://roechling.com/fileadmin/downloads/Roechling_Medical/Brochures/EN/IFU-Bifurcated_Allergy_Skin_Testing_Needle.pdf), 4+ means the wheal was bigger than the histamine control spot. The main scale sets 4+ at the histamine wheal's size plus 2 millimeters, and the alternate scale in the same leaflet calls 4+ anything bigger than histamine but less than twice its size. Clinics don't all use the same scale, so ask which one your report follows.

Q: What is the histamine control on an allergy test?
A: It's a spot that should always react. [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diagnostics/24912-allergy-skin-test) says the positive control contains histamine and raises an itchy wheal shortly after it's applied. If it stays flat, [European skin test standards](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3565910/) point to a problem with how the test was applied or a medicine that's blocking the reaction, so negative results may not be reliable.

Q: What does 0.10 mean on an allergy test?
A: A number like 0.10 comes from an allergy blood test, not a skin test. The [AAFA](https://aafa.org/allergies/allergy-diagnosis/) says blood test results are reported on a scale of 0.1 to 100 kilounits of antibody, so 0.10 sits at the very bottom. [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/treatments/22345-allergy-blood-test) notes that scoring varies between test brands, so your doctor reads it against your lab's own cutoffs.

Q: Is there a fast allergy treatment after a positive allergy test?
A: Allergy medicine is the quickest way to ease symptoms, while immunotherapy treats the allergy itself over a longer course. Allergy shots take three to five years of maintenance after a build-up phase, per the [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/allergy-shots-%28immunotherapy%29). ILIT (intralymphatic immunotherapy) is typically three injections over about two months. To ask whether it fits the allergens on your test, use [Find a Provider](/find-an-ilit-provider) on OpenAirAllergyNetwork.com to locate an allergist who offers it.

On a skin prick test, your allergy test results are the raised, itchy bumps called wheals that appear where allergens went into your skin, and how big each one gets. [European skin test standards](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3565910/) count a wheal of 3 millimeters or more as positive. A positive result shows your immune system has made allergy antibodies to that allergen. It doesn't prove you're allergic to it, which is why an allergist reads your skin prick test results alongside your symptoms.

[Allergy Testing Before Immunotherapy: Skin Test vs. Blood Test](/blog/allergy-skin-test-vs-blood-test "card")

## How to read allergy skin test results

You'll see rows of test spots, usually marked with labels, plus two control spots. [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/lab-tests/allergy-skin-test/) says the test is done on areas such as the forearm or the back.

1. **Check the two control spots first.** [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diagnostics/24912-allergy-skin-test) explains that the positive control is histamine, which should raise an itchy wheal, and the negative control is saline, which shouldn't. The European standards say the saline spot rules out skin that welts from any scratch, and the histamine spot confirms the test was applied correctly and no medicine is blocking the reaction.
2. **Find the wheals.** [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/003519.htm) says results usually show within 15 to 20 minutes. The [Asthma and Allergy Foundation of America](https://aafa.org/allergies/allergy-diagnosis/) (AAFA) describes a wheal as a raised, round area that looks like a hive, and says the spot also gets red, swollen and itchy.
3. **Look at the size.** Your provider measures each wheal with a ruler, Cleveland Clinic says. The European standards measure the widest point of the raised bump, not the redness around it, and call 3 millimeters or more positive.
4. **Match each positive to your life.** The [American College of Allergy, Asthma and Immunology](https://acaai.org/resource/allergy-testing/) (ACAAI) gives two examples: a positive ragweed test only matters if your nose and eyes act up in ragweed season in late summer, and a positive dust mite test only matters if you sneeze or get itchy eyes when you vacuum.

Serious reactions to the test itself are uncommon. MedlinePlus calls a whole body reaction during skin testing very rare, and says your provider will be prepared to treat one.

## Allergy test results scale: what 1+ to 5+ mean

Some reports list millimeters. Others grade each spot on a plus scale that compares it with the histamine wheal. There's no single version. The [instructions for one skin testing device](https://roechling.com/fileadmin/downloads/Roechling_Medical/Brochures/EN/IFU-Bifurcated_Allergy_Skin_Testing_Needle.pdf) give two different grading systems on the same page. Here's the main one, scored after subtracting any wheal at the saline spot:

| Grade | Wheal size compared with histamine |
|---|---|
| 0 | No wheal |
| 1+ | Less than half the histamine wheal |
| 2+ | Half the histamine wheal |
| 3+ | Same size as the histamine wheal |
| 4+ | Histamine wheal plus 2 millimeters |
| 5+ | More than twice the histamine wheal |

In the alternate system, 4+ covers anything bigger than histamine but less than twice its size. If your report uses pluses, ask which scale your clinic follows before you compare it with anyone else's.

## What does a positive allergy test mean?

A positive allergy test means you're sensitized. The AAFA explains that your immune system has made IgE antibodies to the allergen, and those antibodies sometimes trigger symptoms and sometimes don't. It says a positive skin test doesn't diagnose an allergy by itself.

How often a positive turns out to matter depends on the allergen. In a [study of 3,068 patients at 17 European allergy centers](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4215109/), 87% to 89% of positive grass and dust mite tests went with symptoms people noticed around that allergen. For cockroach it was 40%.

### Does a bigger wheal mean a worse allergy?

No. A bigger wheal makes it more likely the allergen is behind your symptoms, but it doesn't measure severity. The AAFA says a larger wheal usually means your body recognizes the allergen more, and that a positive skin test doesn't predict how severe a reaction will be. In the European study, the chance of having symptoms rose with wheal size for 17 of the 18 airborne allergens tested.

## Negative allergy test but still have symptoms?

A negative skin test most often means you aren't allergic to that substance, according to MedlinePlus, though in rare cases a person is allergic anyway. Before you accept a negative, check three things with your allergist:

1. **Did the histamine spot react?** If it stayed flat, a medicine may have blocked the test. The ACAAI says the controls help show whether an interfering medicine was taken by mistake.
2. **Was your trigger on the panel?** The ACAAI says people who test negative to a full panel likely react to a less common allergen or have nonallergic rhinitis, which looks like allergies without an allergic cause.
3. **Is an intradermal test next?** The [American Academy of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/allergy-testing) (AAAAI) says allergists may use this more sensitive test, which places a small amount of allergen just under the skin, when prick tests are negative but an allergy still seems likely.

## Allergy blood test results: numbers and classes

If your allergy test results are numbers in kilounits, like 0.10 or 3.5, they came from a blood test. The AAFA says these are reported on a scale of 0.1 to 100 kilounits of antibody, sometimes grouped into six classes. As with wheal size, a higher number means your body is more likely to recognize the allergen, not that the allergy is worse.

Blood results run high on false positives. [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/treatments/22345-allergy-blood-test) says about 50% to 60% of positive allergy blood tests don't turn out to be an allergy, and that scoring varies from one test brand to another. Our guide to the [allergy skin test vs. blood test](/blog/allergy-skin-test-vs-blood-test) covers when each one is used.

## How your allergy test results shape treatment

Your results become your treatment list. The ACAAI says allergy shots contain tiny, increasing amounts of the allergens you're sensitive to, and the ACAAI adds that shots may help when your symptoms match the allergens your skin test found. When the history and the skin test aren't conclusive, the European standards note that a challenge test, a supervised exposure to a small dose of the allergen, may help settle it before immunotherapy starts.

## Longer term options

Allergy medicine eases symptoms day to day. Immunotherapy works on the allergy itself, using the allergens your test identified.

**Allergy shots (SCIT)** start with a build-up phase of shots once or twice a week for four to six months, then maintenance shots every two to four weeks for three to five years, according to the [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/allergy-shots-%28immunotherapy%29).

**Allergy drops or tablets (SLIT)** go under the tongue. The [ACAAI](https://acaai.org/allergies/treatment/allergy-testing/skin-test) says that whether it's given as shots or under the tongue, immunotherapy uses gradually increasing doses of your allergen.

**ILIT (intralymphatic immunotherapy)** is a much shorter course and, for some people, a faster alternative to allergy shots. An allergist injects FDA approved allergen extract into a lymph node in the groin (just below the waist, near the hip), an off label route, typically three times over about two months. Research is promising but smaller than for allergy shots, and results vary. The [Learn About ILIT](/learn-about-ilit) page covers who may be a candidate.

Our [comparison of allergy shots, allergy drops and ILIT](/learn-about-ilit/allergy-shots-vs-allergy-drops-vs-ilit) lays the options side by side. If you just got your results, an allergist who offers ILIT can review them with your history and tell you which allergens a short course could treat.
