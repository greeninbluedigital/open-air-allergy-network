Houston allergies barely take a break. Cedar pollen drifts in during winter, oak takes over in March, grass carries through summer, and ragweed and cedar elm share the fall. Mold and dust mites keep going in between, because Houston's warm, humid climate lets plants grow and spores spread almost all year, as [Houston Methodist](https://www.houstonmethodist.org/blog/articles/2025/mar/houston-allergies-what-to-know-if-youre-new-to-houston/) medical center explains.

Allergy medicine handles the symptoms while you take it. If you're tired of allergy pills, eye drops and nasal sprays every season, immunotherapy is another option. Allergy shots, allergy drops or tablets, and ILIT (intralymphatic immunotherapy) treat the allergy itself. More on those below.

## Winter allergies in Houston: cedar pollen

[Houston Methodist](https://www.houstonmethodist.org/blog/articles/2025/mar/houston-allergies-what-to-know-if-youre-new-to-houston/) names cedar pollen from Ashe juniper, the tree Texans call mountain cedar, as Houston's main winter allergen. Ashe juniper is the dominant tree cover in the Hill Country west of I-35, according to the [Texas A&M Forest Service](https://stories.tamu.edu/news/2024/12/18/cedar-fever-season-has-arrived-in-texas/), and its pollen can blow for miles. The agency adds that eastern red cedars in eastern parts of the state pollinate around the same time and can cause a similar reaction. Houston's cedar count hit 560 on February 12, 2026, well into the heavy range.

## Spring allergies in Houston: oak pollen

Oak pollen is Houston's major spring allergen. The [Houston Health Department](https://www.houstonhealth.org/Services/pollen-mold/houston-pollen-mold-count-friday-may-12026) measures tree pollen from mid-January through mid-April, and in its [March 2026 counts](https://www.houstonhealth.org/services/pollen-mold) oak made up about three quarters of all the tree pollen. Oak topped 1,500 grains per cubic meter, the extremely heavy mark for tree pollen, on eight March days, peaking at 3,298 on March 16.

The department's 2026 counts also picked up pine, pecan, ash and mulberry in that window. Houston Methodist adds maple, cottonwood, birch and mesquite to its list of spring tree pollens. Our [tree pollen allergy guide](/blog/tree-pollen-allergy) covers symptoms and treatment.

## Summer allergies in Houston: grass, mold and hurricane season

Grass pollen showed up in the city's count every month from January through October 2026 and peaked in May. Grass counts look small next to oak, but the department's scale treats 20 grass grains per cubic meter as heavy, so a count in the 20s can still mean a bad day.

Mold is a problem most of the year: the department's mold count reached the heavy range in eight of the first ten months of 2026, every month but February and August. The [AAFA](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/aafa-2026-allergy-capitals-report.pdf) says mold levels tend to be highest in the Southeast because of its warm, humid climate, and spore counts have been reported to rise before, during and after storms. Hurricane season runs June 1 to November 30, and Houston Methodist notes that a hurricane's aftermath is ideal for mold, pollen and dust mites. After a flood, the [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/mold-health/about/clean-up.html) says people with allergies or asthma shouldn't take part in mold cleanup, and anyone who does should wear at least an N95 respirator. Have someone without allergies handle it if you can.

## Fall allergies in Houston: ragweed and cedar elm

Ragweed is Houston's fall pollen. In the [health department's](https://www.houstonhealth.org/services/pollen-mold) 2026 records it started climbing in September and reached 544 on October 23, above the 500 mark the department calls extremely heavy for weeds. The AAFA says ragweed symptoms can last until the first frost kills the plant, and Houston's winters barely dip below freezing. Our [ragweed allergy guide](/blog/ragweed-allergy) covers symptoms and treatment.

Cedar elm pollen overlaps with ragweed. The department [measures cedar elm pollen](https://www.houstonhealth.org/Services/pollen-mold/houston-pollen-mold-count-friday-may-12026) in September and October, and the elm count hit 876 on October 9, 2026. If your fall allergies don't fit ragweed's timing, ask your allergist to test for elm too.

## Why are allergies so bad in Houston?

Houston's summers are long, hot and often muggy, and its winters barely dip below freezing, [Houston Methodist](https://www.houstonmethodist.org/blog/articles/2025/mar/houston-allergies-what-to-know-if-youre-new-to-houston/) explains, so plants keep growing and pollinating for most of the year. The humidity also lets dust mites and mold thrive year-round. For people allergic to those, symptoms can run all year.

Dr. Ashish Asawa, a Houston Methodist allergist, says newcomers with a history of allergies commonly become sensitive to the region's allergens after two or more years in Houston.

## How Houston ranks for allergies

Last place is good news here. The AAFA's Allergy Capitals report ranks the 100 largest U.S. metros from the most challenging place to live with allergies (1st) to the least (100th). In the [2026 report](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/aafa-2026-allergy-capitals-report.pdf), Houston came in 100th, with a score of 56.21 against an average of 80.63, and the report rated it better than average for pollen and for allergy medicine use.

One easier year doesn't make Houston easy on allergies. It ranked 14th in 2024, according to Houston Methodist, and 26th in the [2025 report](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/aafa-2025-allergy-capitals-report.pdf). The ranking counts days with high pollen readings and leaves mold out entirely, and the AAFA itself says a person could live in its 100th city and still have significant allergies. For the national picture, see [the worst U.S. cities for allergies in 2026](/blog/worst-cities-for-allergies-2026).

## Where to find the Houston pollen count

The [Houston Health Department](https://www.houstonhealth.org/services/pollen-mold) posts free pollen and mold counts Monday through Friday, except City holidays, and keeps a recorded message at 713-247-5846. The counts come from a [spore trap on the roof of its lab](https://www.houstonhealth.org/Services/pollen-mold/houston-pollen-mold-count-friday-may-12026) that samples air around the clock, and its ranges are based on the National Allergy Bureau's data. Each daily report breaks the total down by tree, weed and mold type, so you can see whether oak, ragweed or mold is behind a bad day.

## How to cut your exposure in Houston

**1. Check the daily count.** On heavy days, keep windows and doors closed and limit time outdoors, the [Texas A&M Forest Service](https://stories.tamu.edu/news/2024/12/18/cedar-fever-season-has-arrived-in-texas/) advises.

**2. Keep windows closed.** The [AAFA](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/aafa-2026-allergy-capitals-report.pdf) recommends closing windows during pollen season or at peak times, usually the afternoon. Run central air or an air cleaner with a HEPA filter if you can.

**3. Rinse pollen off before bed.** Shower before bed and change clothes after time outdoors, the AAFA advises, and dry laundry in a dryer or on an indoor rack, not on an outdoor line.

**4. Keep indoor humidity between 30% and 50%.** The AAFA suggests checking with a hygrometer and running a dehumidifier if needed. It also suggests fixing leaks quickly and clearing dead leaves from the yard. Our [dust mite allergy guide](/blog/dust-mite-allergy) covers the bedroom steps that help with mites.

## Allergy testing in Houston

With cedar, oak, grass, ragweed, elm, mold and dust mites all in the mix, symptoms alone rarely tell you which one is getting to you. A skin or blood test can, and that matters if you're considering immunotherapy, which is matched to your specific allergens. Our guide to [allergy skin tests vs. blood tests](/blog/allergy-skin-test-vs-blood-test) explains how the two compare, and [Allergy Test Results Explained](/blog/allergy-test-results) walks through what the numbers mean.

## Longer term options for Houston allergies

Allergy medicine controls symptoms while you take it, but it doesn't change the allergy. Houston Methodist notes that antihistamines act faster than steroid nasal sprays, which may take a couple of days to help. If medicine isn't enough, immunotherapy builds tolerance to an allergen over time, and it comes in three forms.

**Allergy shots (SCIT)** are given at the allergist's office, not at home. The [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/allergy-shots-%28immunotherapy%29) describes shots once or twice a week for four to six months during build-up, then every two to four weeks for three to five years. Our [guide to allergy shots](/learn-about-ilit/allergy-shots-and-allergy-immunotherapy) explains the schedule.

**Allergy drops and tablets (SLIT)** dissolve under the tongue at home. Our [comparison of allergy shots, allergy drops and ILIT](/learn-about-ilit/allergy-shots-vs-allergy-drops-vs-ilit) covers which allergens each one treats.

**ILIT** is the shortest course, and for some people a faster alternative to allergy shots. An allergist injects FDA approved allergen extract into a lymph node in the groin, near the hip, guided by ultrasound. Giving the extract that way is an off label use. A typical course is about three injections over about two months, the schedule used in the [2008 trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2582048/) that compared it with three years of shots for grass pollen allergy. ILIT research is promising but smaller than the research behind allergy shots, and results vary from person to person. Our [ILIT FDA FAQ](/learn-about-ilit#faq-fda) covers the FDA question in more detail.
