FAQS
Q: Is Orlando bad for allergies?
A: It's tougher than average. The AAFA ranked Orlando 33rd of the 100 largest metro areas in the contiguous U.S. in its [2026 Allergy Capitals report](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/aafa-2026-allergy-capitals-report.pdf), up from 34th in [2025](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/aafa-2025-allergy-capitals-report.pdf). Orlando's pollen score was average in the 2026 report, while sales of over the counter allergy medicine scored worse than average.

Q: When is allergy season in Central Florida?
A: Tree season comes first. A [UF/IFAS fact sheet](https://ask.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/FR268/pdf) says Florida trees often release pollen from January to June. Grasses pollinate in spring, summer and fall, according to [UF/IFAS Gardening Solutions](https://gardeningsolutions.ifas.ufl.edu/?p=24975), and ragweed pollen peaks from July to November, according to the UF/IFAS [Mid-Florida Research and Education Center](https://mrec.ifas.ufl.edu/resources/weedsbyflowercolor/green/ambrosiaartemisiifolia/).

Q: When does allergy season end in Central Florida?
A: There's no clean end. Ragweed's peak runs through November, and [UF/IFAS Gardening Solutions](https://gardeningsolutions.ifas.ufl.edu/?p=24975) says some areas see high oak pollen counts as early as December, so the quiet stretch can be short. Mold and dust mites can keep symptoms going indoors all year.

Q: What is causing allergies in Central Florida right now?
A: It depends on the month. Late winter and spring point to tree pollen, especially oak. Summer brings grass pollen and mold, and late summer through fall adds ragweed. For today's levels, check a pollen count from a monitoring site, which the [AAFA](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/aafa-2026-allergy-capitals-report.pdf) says is more accurate than a forecast.

Q: Does rain help allergies or make them worse?
A: It can do both. Rain from frequent thunderstorms naturally suppresses pollen, according to [UF/IFAS](https://blogs.ifas.ufl.edu/news/2026/03/17/the-unwelcome-dust-that-wont-quit-pollen-season-hits-with-full-force/), so a rainy day can bring relief from pollen. Mold works the other way: the [AAFA](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/aafa-2026-allergy-capitals-report.pdf) says rain and rising humidity drive mold growth, and airborne mold spores have been reported to climb before, during and after storms.

Q: Do palm trees cause allergies?
A: Usually not. [UF/IFAS Gardening Solutions](https://gardeningsolutions.ifas.ufl.edu/?p=24975) lists palm trees among the trees that get blamed unfairly for allergies. One exception on its list of trees scored for allergy potential is the date palm, which scores 9 on the 1 to 10 allergy scale it uses.

Q: Can you develop allergies after moving to Florida?
A: Yes, it can happen. The [AAFA](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/aafa-2026-allergy-capitals-report.pdf) says that even if you move away from your old local pollen, you could develop allergies to the pollens where you live now. Florida's mix is its own, with bahiagrass as the state's most abundant grass and 11 oak species, according to [UF/IFAS Gardening Solutions](https://gardeningsolutions.ifas.ufl.edu/?p=24975).

Q: Are there faster options than allergy shots in Orlando?
A: Yes. Allergy shots usually start with a build-up of four to six months, and maintenance shots typically continue for three to five years, according to the [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/allergy-shots-%28immunotherapy%29). ILIT (intralymphatic immunotherapy) is a much shorter course of about three injections over about two months. If it interests you, use [Find a Provider](/find-an-ilit-provider) on OpenAirAllergyNetwork.com to locate an allergist who offers it.

Orlando allergies run for most of the year. Oak pollen leads in late winter and spring, grasses carry on from spring into fall, ragweed peaks from July to November, and mold and dust mites thrive in Central Florida's warm, wet summers. The Asthma and Allergy Foundation of America (AAFA) ranked the Orlando metro area 33rd of the 100 largest metro areas in the contiguous U.S. in its [2026 Allergy Capitals report](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/aafa-2026-allergy-capitals-report.pdf), worse than average overall.

For the long term, immunotherapy treats the allergy itself, and ILIT is a fast allergy treatment course compared with years of shots.

Match your worst weeks to the table, then take that list to an allergist. It's a quick way to narrow down what to test for.

## Why are my allergies so bad in Orlando?

Trees produce about 80% of the airborne pollen in Florida, according to [UF/IFAS Gardening Solutions](https://gardeningsolutions.ifas.ufl.edu/?p=24975), and the AAFA notes that warmer, more humid climates in the Southeast support pollen production. Something is in the air for much of the year.

Orlando placed 33rd in the AAFA's 2026 ranking and 34th in its [2025 report](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/aafa-2025-allergy-capitals-report.pdf). The 2026 score blends three things: pollen, sales of over the counter allergy medicine and the number of allergists. Orlando's pollen came in average for the year. Its over the counter allergy medicine sales, measured against the estimated number of people with allergies, scored worse than average. The AAFA points out one limit: that medicine category includes decongestants, which some people buy for colds.

## Spring: oak season in Orlando's drier months

![Southern live oaks with long arching limbs covered in ferns and Spanish moss over a paved path lined with palmettos](https://res.cloudinary.com/qruprn0t/image/upload/v1793663949/orlando-allergies-southern-live-oak-spanish-moss.jpg "Trees produce about 80% of the airborne pollen in Florida. Oak is one of the most allergenic.")

[UF/IFAS Gardening Solutions](https://gardeningsolutions.ifas.ufl.edu/?p=24975) calls oak one of the most allergenic trees and says Florida's 11 oak species peak from February to May. The species take turns. Laurel oaks start first, and a UF/IFAS urban forester told [UF/IFAS News](https://blogs.ifas.ufl.edu/news/2026/03/17/the-unwelcome-dust-that-wont-quit-pollen-season-hits-with-full-force/) in March 2026 that live oaks and cedars would follow over the next month. Eastern red cedar, bald cypress and wax myrtle are also highly allergenic and common in Florida, UF/IFAS Gardening Solutions says.

Spring is also one of Orlando's drier stretches. The [National Weather Service](https://www.weather.gov/mlb/normals) normals for Orlando average about 3 inches of rain in March and 2.6 in April, compared with about 8 in June. That matters because rain helps clear pollen from the air. In 2024, an Orlando Health pediatrician told [Central Florida Public Media](https://www.cfpublic.org/health/2024-03-21/pollen-central-florida-doctors-seeing-more-allergy-complaints) that spring rain usually helps wash out pollen, and a dry spring that year left it hanging around. The station reported that oak catkins had been prolific in the weeks before. AdventHealth told the station its Centra Care locations saw allergy patients double in the second week of March. The AAFA makes the same point about drought: with no rain to wash pollen out of the air, counts can stay high for days at a time.

**Tip:** UF/IFAS agents suggest limiting time outdoors from 10 a.m. to 4 p.m. and on windy days, and wearing a mask when you mow or garden.

## Summer and fall: grass, ragweed and the rainy season

Grass takes over as tree pollen fades. [UF/IFAS Gardening Solutions](https://gardeningsolutions.ifas.ufl.edu/?p=24975) calls bahiagrass the most abundant grass in Florida and ranks it high on the list of allergenic plants, and it says Bermudagrass pollinates year round in the state's subtropical areas. Florida's grass season is long. The [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/mowing-down-your-grass-allergies) says grass in most of the U.S. usually pollinates from April through early June, while UF/IFAS says Florida grasses pollinate in spring, summer and fall.

Ragweed is the fall weed to know. The UF/IFAS [Mid-Florida Research and Education Center](https://mrec.ifas.ufl.edu/resources/weedsbyflowercolor/green/ambrosiaartemisiifolia/), in nearby Apopka, describes its pollen as highly allergenic and gives a peak season of July to November. Fall weed pollen varies from year to year. The AAFA found very low fall weed pollen in many Florida and Texas cities in 2025 and suggested flooding may have washed away the weeds' seed base. Our [ragweed allergy guide](/blog/ragweed-allergy) covers symptoms and the foods that can cross react with it.

Summer is also when Orlando gets most of its rain. The [National Weather Service](https://www.weather.gov/mlb/normals) normals add up to about 23 inches from June through August, close to half of the year's roughly 51 inches. Warm temperatures, rain and rising humidity drive mold growth, the [AAFA](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/aafa-2026-allergy-capitals-report.pdf) says, and mold levels tend to be highest in the Southeast. Mold isn't counted in the Allergy Capitals rankings, so it's easy to miss.

**Tip:** If you mow, wear a mask, then shower and change clothes afterward. Grass pollen is light and travels on the wind, the AAFA says, so pollen from grass beyond your own yard can still reach you.

## Indoors all year: dust mites and mold

Dust mites like Orlando's climate. The [AAFA](https://aafa.org/allergies/types-of-allergies/insect-allergy/dust-mite-allergy/) says they live in nearly every home and thrive at around 70°F and above with humidity over 50%. Orlando's normal average temperature stays above 70°F every month from April through October, according to the National Weather Service, so conditions suit them for much of the year.

Mold can grow indoors after water damage, too. The [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/mold-health/about/index.html) says people with a mold allergy or asthma may have severe reactions, and it advises drying out a home fully within 24 to 48 hours after a flood.

**Tip:** Keep indoor humidity at 50% or below, as the CDC advises. Start dust mite control in the bedroom, where the AAFA says there are usually more mites than anywhere else in the home. Our [dust mite allergy guide](/blog/dust-mite-allergy) walks through the steps.

## What probably isn't to blame

Pine is easy to blame because you can see it. A WGCU and Florida Public Radio Emergency Network [report](https://www.wgcu.org/section/weather/2026-01-12/high-pollen-levels-especially-from-trees-reported-across-state) says pine pollen typically peaks in Central Florida in February. [UF/IFAS Gardening Solutions](https://gardeningsolutions.ifas.ufl.edu/?p=24975) says pine is known for laying on thick yellow dust, but it calls pine pollen low allergenic. The oak pollen arriving at the same time is the more likely trigger.

Palm trees, mango, Brazilian pepper and melaleuca are among the trees UF/IFAS says get blamed unfairly. In general, plants that rely on insects to carry their pollen don't need to make as much of it, UF/IFAS explains.

## How to cut your allergy exposure in Orlando

These steps come from the [AAFA](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/aafa-2026-allergy-capitals-report.pdf), UF/IFAS and the Orlando Health pediatrician quoted above.

**1. Check a pollen count on bad days.** The AAFA says counts from monitoring sites are more accurate than forecasts. On high days, stay inside when you can, and wear sunglasses and a mask when you go out.

**2. Keep windows shut and the air conditioning on.** The AAFA recommends closed windows during pollen season and central air or a HEPA air cleaner. Change the air filters in your home and car, the Orlando Health pediatrician advised.

**3. Rinse off after time outdoors.** Change clothes and wash your hair when you come in, and shower before bed so pollen stays out of your bedding.

**4. Dry laundry inside.** Clothes on an outdoor line collect pollen. Use the dryer or an indoor rack.

**5. Start medicine early.** The AAFA suggests starting allergy medicine a couple of weeks before your allergy season begins, which in Central Florida can mean December or January for oak.

## Allergy testing in Orlando

Oak, grass, ragweed, mold and dust mites can all cause the same sneezing, stuffy nose and itchy eyes, so symptoms alone won't tell you which one is the problem. A skin test or blood test can show what you react to, and that's the first step toward immunotherapy, which is matched to your allergens. ## Longer term options for Orlando allergies

Allergy medicine controls symptoms while you take it, but it doesn't change the allergy. If medicine doesn't give you complete relief, the AAFA suggests talking with an allergist about immunotherapy, which can change how your immune system responds to allergens.

**Allergy shots (SCIT)** have the longest schedule. The [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/allergy-shots-%28immunotherapy%29) describes shots once or twice a week for four to six months during build-up, then every two to four weeks for three to five years. Our [guide to allergy shots](/learn-about-ilit/allergy-shots-and-allergy-immunotherapy) explains the schedule.

**Allergy tablets and drops (SLIT)** go under the tongue. The [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/mowing-down-your-grass-allergies) says grass tablets are approved in the U.S. and are taken at home after the first doses in the allergist's office. Our [comparison of allergy shots, allergy drops and ILIT](/learn-about-ilit/allergy-shots-vs-allergy-drops-vs-ilit) covers which allergens each one treats.

**ILIT (intralymphatic immunotherapy)** is the shortest course, and for some people a faster alternative to allergy shots. An allergist uses ultrasound to inject FDA approved allergen extract into a lymph node in the groin (just below the waist, near the hip). Giving the extract that way is an off label use. A typical course is about three injections over about two months, the schedule from a [2008 trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2582048/) in people with grass pollen allergy. In that study, ILIT raised nasal tolerance to grass pollen within four months, and after three years the effect wasn't significantly different from three years of allergy shots, with fewer allergic side effects.

ILIT research is promising but smaller than the research behind allergy shots, and results vary from person to person. The [ILIT FDA FAQ](/learn-about-ilit#faq-fda) covers the off label question in more detail.
