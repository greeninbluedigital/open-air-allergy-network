KEY TAKEAWAYS
- An allergy shot is a diluted extract of the things you're allergic to, mixed for you from your test results.
- The rest of the vial is mostly salt water, glycerin and a small amount of phenol, a preservative.
- Swelling where the shot went in is the most common side effect, and it's usually gone within 48 hours.
- Serious whole body reactions are rare and often start within 30 minutes, which is why you wait 30 minutes in the office after every shot.
- ILIT uses FDA approved allergen extracts too, and in a 2008 trial its three injections added up to a far smaller total dose than three years of shots.

FAQS
Q: What is allergy shot serum?
A: "Serum" is the everyday name for the vial your allergist mixes for you. [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/allergy-shot-reactions/) describes it as a cocktail of allergens tailored to your own allergies. It's made from licensed allergen extracts, and concentrates are diluted with sterile fluid before the first dose, as the [extract labels](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=4a853ed7-32f7-44bf-bea1-3e90994e7651) require.

Q: Do allergy shots contain aluminum?
A: Some do. One FDA licensed product line, Center-Al, binds pollen and other extracts to aluminum hydroxide, which its [label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=71a32d27-68d0-459a-908d-c66301f2abd2) says is thought to release the allergen more slowly. The same label notes a theoretical risk from aluminum for people with kidney problems, Alzheimer's disease or Down syndrome, so ask which type of extract your clinic uses if that applies to you.

Q: Do allergy shots contain preservatives?
A: Often, yes. The extract labels from [ALK-Abelló](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=cbe1d24d-df77-4940-baf8-623993fa731b) and [Nelco Laboratories](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=4a853ed7-32f7-44bf-bea1-3e90994e7651) list 0.4% phenol as the preservative. The salt water diluent called for on the [Center-Al label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=71a32d27-68d0-459a-908d-c66301f2abd2) carries the same 0.4% phenol.

Q: What are the long term side effects of allergy shots?
A: [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/treatments/25194-allergy-shots) says allergy shots have no long-term risks or complications. The risks it does name are reactions shortly after a shot, which is why each visit ends with a 30 minute wait.

Q: When should I worry about an allergy shot reaction?
A: Worry when symptoms show up away from the injection site. [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/allergy-shot-reactions/) lists trouble breathing, chest tightness or wheezing, dizziness or fainting, and hives away from the shot as signs of a serious reaction. If that happens after you leave, use your epinephrine autoinjector if you were given one, and return to the clinic or go to an emergency room, as [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/tests-procedures/allergy-shots/about/pac-20392876) advises.

Q: How long do allergy shot reactions last?
A: Swelling at the injection site usually goes away within 48 hours, according to [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/allergy-shot-reactions/). A delayed local reaction can start several hours after the shot and is usually at its worst around 24 hours, per the [extract label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=cbe1d24d-df77-4940-baf8-623993fa731b), so a sore, itchy arm the next day can happen. A whole body reaction the next day would be extremely unusual, the allergist quoted by Cleveland Clinic says.

Q: Can allergy shots cause weight gain?
A: Weight gain isn't among the side effects on the [extract label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=cbe1d24d-df77-4940-baf8-623993fa731b), which lists local and whole body allergic reactions, and no steroid appears among the ingredients. Weight gain can be a side effect of steroid shots such as Kenalog, a different drug covered in our [Kenalog guide](/blog/kenalog-shot-for-allergies).

Q: Do allergy shots weaken your immune system?
A: No. They shift how your immune system responds to specific allergens, from inflammation toward tolerance, as [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/treatments/25194-allergy-shots) explains. A [U.S. surveillance study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33753219/) of allergy practices found no infections from the shots in the years it checked (2014 to 2018) and concluded they aren't linked to a higher risk of infections.

Q: Do ILIT allergy shots use the same extracts?
A: Yes, in a smaller amount. ILIT uses FDA approved allergen extracts injected into a lymph node, an off label route our [ILIT FAQ](/learn-about-ilit#faq-fda) explains. In a [2008 trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19001265/), three ILIT injections delivered a total allergen dose of 3,000 units, against more than 4 million over 54 allergy shots. If you'd like to ask about it, use [Find a Provider](/find-an-ilit-provider) on OpenAirAllergyNetwork.com to locate an allergist who offers it.

If you've wondered what's in allergy shots, or what they're made of, before committing to years of them, the answer is personal: each shot holds a diluted extract of the pollens, dust mites, molds, pet dander or insect venoms your tests showed you react to, mixed into sterile salt water with glycerin and a small amount of preservative. The extract labels list no steroid or antihistamine among the ingredients, and the side effects people get, most often a swollen, itchy spot on the arm, mostly come from the allergen itself.

Once you know what's in the vial, the side effects are easier to follow, so below you'll find what each part of the vial does, which allergy shot side effects are common and which are rare, what to do about a reaction, and how the shorter options compare, from allergy drops and tablets to ILIT (intralymphatic immunotherapy), a faster alternative to allergy shots that uses the same kind of extracts.

[How Often Do You Get Allergy Shots? A Busy Person's Guide](/blog/how-often-do-you-get-allergy-shots "card")

## When to get care right away

Hives away from the injection site, throat tightness, wheezing, chest tightness, shortness of breath, dizziness or fainting after an allergy shot can mean a serious reaction, according to the [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/allergy-shots-(immunotherapy)) and [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/allergy-shot-reactions/). If it starts after you leave the office, use your epinephrine autoinjector if you were prescribed one, then return to the clinic or go to an emergency room, as [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/tests-procedures/allergy-shots/about/pac-20392876) advises.

## What's in allergy shots: the ingredients

The active ingredient is allergen extract. FDA labels describe it as the extractable parts of pollens, molds, animal dander, insects and other sources, and your allergist mixes the extracts that match your test results into one vial. [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/allergy-shot-reactions/) calls it a cocktail of allergens tailored to your allergies, which is why patients often call the vial their "serum." The rest of the liquid dilutes and preserves the extract.

| Ingredient | What it does |
|---|---|
| Allergen extract | The part that treats you. Each shot has just enough allergen to alert the immune system without causing symptoms |
| Sterile salt water (saline) | Dilutes the extract to your dose. Some extracts use Coca's fluid, which is salt water with a little sodium bicarbonate as a buffer. One diluent option adds albumin, a protein that stabilizes the diluted extract |
| Glycerin | Many concentrated extracts are 50% glycerin. It can cause some discomfort as the shot goes in |
| Phenol (0.4%) | A preservative |
| Aluminum hydroxide (alum precipitated products only, such as Center-Al) | Binds the allergen, which is thought to release it more slowly |
| Sodium hydroxide and hydrochloric acid | Listed among the inactive ingredients on the ALK-Abelló and Center-Al labels |

*Sources: FDA extract labels from [ALK-Abelló](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=cbe1d24d-df77-4940-baf8-623993fa731b), [Nelco Laboratories](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=4a853ed7-32f7-44bf-bea1-3e90994e7651) and [Center-Al](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=71a32d27-68d0-459a-908d-c66301f2abd2), and [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/tests-procedures/allergy-shots/about/pac-20392876).*

The [Nelco label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=4a853ed7-32f7-44bf-bea1-3e90994e7651) requires diluting concentrated extracts with sterile fluid before the first use on a patient, so treatment starts with a weak dilution and your dose climbs to stronger concentrations over the build-up months.

What isn't in the vial: none of these labels lists a steroid or an antihistamine among its ingredients. A "one-time" steroid shot such as Kenalog is a different drug, a corticosteroid, covered in our [Kenalog guide](/blog/kenalog-shot-for-allergies).

## Allergy shots side effects: the common ones

Reactions where the needle went in are the most common side effect. [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/tests-procedures/allergy-shots/about/pac-20392876) describes swelling, irritation or a change in skin color that usually starts within a few hours and clears up soon after. The [extract label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=cbe1d24d-df77-4940-baf8-623993fa731b) adds that some local reactions are delayed: swelling, redness, itching or pain that starts hours later, peaks around 24 hours and usually needs no treatment beyond an antihistamine by mouth.

[Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/allergy-shot-reactions/) says normal injection site swelling can range from pea sized to golf ball sized and is usually gone within 48 hours. A very large swelling right after the shot is worth mentioning before you leave, since the label says it can be the first sign of a reaction spreading through the body.

Mild whole body symptoms such as a stuffy nose, sneezing or hives can also happen, per [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/treatments/25194-allergy-shots).

## Serious reactions: how rare are they?

Severe whole body reactions are rare, and deaths are exceedingly rare, according to the [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/allergy-shots-(immunotherapy)). Allergy specialist Mark Aronica, MD, told [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/allergy-shot-reactions/) that serious reactions are generally considered to be less than 1%. A [U.S. surveillance study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33753219/) built on yearly surveys of ACAAI and AAAAI members gathered data on 64.5 million injection visits from 2008 to 2018 and counted one fatal reaction for every 7.2 million visits.

The same study found that asthma, especially severe asthma, was a major risk factor, and half of the most severe reactions happened in people with severe asthma. Practices that timed each patient's wait and had them check out with staff had significantly fewer severe reactions. [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/tests-procedures/allergy-shots/about/pac-20392876) says anaphylaxis often begins within 30 minutes of a shot, which is why everyone waits that long in the office, though it sometimes starts later.

## Long term side effects of allergy shots

[Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/treatments/25194-allergy-shots) says allergy shots have no long-term side effects. The shots shift your immune response to specific allergens toward tolerance, and the surveillance study above found no infections from the shots in the years it checked (2014 to 2018). The long-term effects the [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/allergy-shots-(immunotherapy)) mentions are good ones: shots may help prevent new allergies, and in children may help reduce the risk of asthma.

## How to lower your risk of a reaction

1. **Stay in the office for 30 minutes after every shot.** Anaphylaxis, a severe whole body reaction, often starts within 30 minutes of an allergy shot, and staying lets the office treat it right away ([Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/tests-procedures/allergy-shots/about/pac-20392876)).
2. **Speak up before the shot if you're unwell.** [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/treatments/25194-allergy-shots) says to hold off with a fever of 100°F or higher, a cold or flu, or an asthma attack, and the [Nelco label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=4a853ed7-32f7-44bf-bea1-3e90994e7651) also lists heavy allergen exposure before a visit as a reason to lower or skip the dose.
3. **Keep asthma under control.** Asthma is a major risk factor in the surveillance data, and the allergist quoted by [Cleveland Clinic](https://health.clevelandclinic.org/allergy-shot-reactions/) advises staying on your asthma medicines so it's well controlled before each shot.
4. **Tell your allergist about blood pressure and heart medicines.** Beta blockers can keep epinephrine from working in some people, according to [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/treatments/25194-allergy-shots).
5. **Ask about an antihistamine beforehand.** [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/tests-procedures/allergy-shots/about/pac-20392876) says one taken before the shot can lower the risk of a reaction, particularly a local one.
6. **Skip hard exercise afterward.** [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/tests-procedures/allergy-shots/about/pac-20392876) advises against vigorous exercise for at least a few hours after a shot.
7. **Stay on schedule.** Missed doses may mean dropping back to a lower dose to prevent a serious reaction, according to [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/tests-procedures/allergy-shots/about/pac-20392876).

## Longer term options

**Allergy medicine** treats symptoms as they come. [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/tests-procedures/allergy-shots/about/pac-20392876) suggests considering allergy shots when medicines don't control symptoms well or cause bothersome side effects.

**Allergy shots** use the extracts above and usually run three to five years, per [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/tests-procedures/allergy-shots/about/pac-20392876). Our guide to [how often you get allergy shots](/blog/how-often-do-you-get-allergy-shots) counts the visits.

**Allergy tablets** dissolve under the tongue. [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/treatments/25194-allergy-shots) lists the FDA approved ones: Odactra for dust mites, Oralair and Grastek for grass, and Ragwitek for short ragweed.

**ILIT allergy treatment** uses FDA approved allergen extracts injected into a lymph node, usually in the groin (just below the waist, near the hip), an off label route our [ILIT FAQ](/learn-about-ilit#faq-fda) explains. It's typically three injections over about two months, which makes it a fast allergy treatment course, though not instant relief. In a [2008 trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19001265/) of 165 people with grass pollen allergy, the three ILIT injections added up to a total allergen dose more than 1,000 times smaller than 54 allergy shots over three years, and ILIT caused fewer side effects. The research is promising but smaller than for allergy shots, and results vary from person to person. Our [ILIT vs. traditional allergy shots](/blog/ilit-vs-traditional-allergy-shots) article covers its side effects.

The [comparison of allergy shots, allergy drops and ILIT](/learn-about-ilit/allergy-shots-vs-allergy-drops-vs-ilit) puts all three side by side, and you can [find an ILIT provider near you](/find-an-ilit-provider) by zip code.
