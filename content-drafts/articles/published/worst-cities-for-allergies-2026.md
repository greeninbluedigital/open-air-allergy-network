The worst cities for allergies in the U.S. in 2026 are led by Boise, Idaho, followed by San Diego and Tulsa, according to the Asthma and Allergy Foundation of America's [2026 Allergy Capitals report](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/aafa-2026-allergy-capitals-report.pdf). It's Boise's first time at No. 1. A year earlier it ranked 95th. ## How the AAFA ranks the worst cities for allergies

The AAFA scores the 100 most populated metro areas in the lower 48 states on three things: tree, grass and weed pollen, over the counter allergy medicine use, and the number of board certified allergists. Pollen scores count how many days in 2025 a metro's automated sensors recorded "high" or "very high" levels, with very high days weighted more. Medicine use is over the counter sinus and allergy medicine sales per 10,000 allergy patients, and specialists are measured per patient population. The factors aren't weighted equally.

Each factor gets a marker of worse than average, average or better than average, set half a standard deviation above or below the average of all 100 metros. So "worse than average" means worse than most big U.S. metros, not a medical cutoff. The ranking also uses the previous year's pollen, which means the 2026 list reflects the 2025 season. Mold isn't counted.

## Why so many Western cities jumped

The top Allergy Capitals have mostly been in the Southeast, where warm, humid weather supports heavy pollen production, according to the [AAFA](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/aafa-2026-allergy-capitals-report.pdf). In 2025 the West had more pollen spikes and a longer pollen season, especially for grass and weed pollen. Several Western cities jumped sharply in the 2026 ranking as a result, and the AAFA says some made the top 20 for the first time ever.

Eight Western metros went from better than average overall in 2025 to worse than average in 2026: Boise, San Diego, Spokane, Provo, Ogden, Salt Lake City, Colorado Springs and Denver. Seven of them now sit in the top 15, and Denver climbed from 91st to 30th. Six of this year's top 10 were in the bottom 20 the year before.

The AAFA ties the jump to the weather. Atmospheric rivers, long bands of water vapor that carry ocean moisture inland, soaked parts of the West in late 2024, and well watered plants produce more pollen the next spring. Warmer temperatures brought spring earlier and pushed the first frost later. In Utah, drought kept pollen hanging in the air, with no rain to wash it out. The AAFA says climate change is behind all of it, making allergy seasons longer and more severe.

## 1. Boise, Idaho: the worst city for allergies in 2026

Boise jumped from 95th to 1st, with a total score of 100.00, the highest on the list. The [AAFA](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/aafa-2026-allergy-capitals-report.pdf) says tree pollen peaked earlier there in 2025, grass pollen started earlier and lasted longer, and weed pollen lasted longer and peaked higher. After a wet 2024 to 2025 winter, Boise's trees produced unusually heavy pollen loads that spring. Boise also scored worse than average for its number of allergists, and the AAFA lists that as part of its jump, as it does for Ogden.

## 2. San Diego, California

San Diego went from 97th to 2nd. An atmospheric river storm brought rain to Central and Southern California in late 2024, and the next year, the AAFA reports, tree pollen peaked earlier while grass and weed pollen spiked well above the year before. Allergy medicine use scored better than average, so San Diego's rank comes mostly from its pollen.

## 3. Tulsa, Oklahoma

Tulsa ranked 4th last year and 3rd this year, and in 2026 both its pollen and its allergy medicine use scored worse than average. Trees dominate Tulsa's air. A [University of Tulsa study](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/allergy/articles/10.3389/falgy.2021.726445/full) of pollen counts from 1996 to 2020 found tree pollen made up about 75% of the total, with oak the single most common type. Tree pollen increased significantly over that span, and pollen from the juniper and cypress family, which includes the mountain cedar behind [cedar fever](/blog/cedar-fever-mountain-cedar-allergy), roughly tripled.

## 4. Provo, Utah

Provo rose from 94th to 4th. The [AAFA](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/aafa-2026-allergy-capitals-report.pdf) says an unusually warm year melted Utah's snowpack early, so trees released pollen early. A heat wave and no rain followed, and by April 2025 the governor had declared a drought emergency. Drought doesn't necessarily make plants produce more pollen, the AAFA notes, but it keeps the pollen in the air longer. Then Utah had its wettest October on record, and weed pollen surged. Provo also scored worse than average for its number of allergists.

## 5. Rochester, New York

Rochester climbed from 85th to 5th. Pollen was its only factor rated worse than average. Medicine use was average, and its supply of allergists was better than average. The AAFA's report doesn't single out a cause for Rochester's jump.

## 6. Wichita, Kansas

Wichita held the No. 1 spot for three years in a row through 2025, according to the [2025 report](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/aafa-2025-allergy-capitals-report.pdf). In 2026 it fell to 6th, and its pollen actually scored better than average. What keeps Wichita high is heavy allergy medicine use and a short supply of allergists, both worse than average. It's a good example of the AAFA's point that a city can rank high for any one of its factors.

## 7. Raleigh, North Carolina

Raleigh held at 7th and is the only top 10 city rated worse than average on all three factors. Raleigh's famous spring "yellow dust" is mostly pine pollen, and [NC State Extension](https://forestry.ces.ncsu.edu/news/pine-pollen-season) says pine pollen is less allergenic than the tiny grains from hardwoods such as oak and hickory, which you can't see. Tree pollen there can start as early as February. Our [tree pollen allergy guide](/blog/tree-pollen-allergy) covers which trees cause the most symptoms.

## 8. Ogden, Utah

Ogden went from 92nd to 8th for the same reasons as Provo: early tree pollen, a long grass season, and weed pollen that started earlier, lasted longer and peaked higher, according to the [AAFA](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/aafa-2026-allergy-capitals-report.pdf). Like Boise, Ogden has fewer allergists than average, which the AAFA lists as part of its jump.

## 9. Spokane, Washington

Spokane rose from 82nd to 9th. The same atmospheric rivers that soaked Boise pushed moisture into Spokane, and the AAFA says grasses across the Pacific Northwest released more pollen, earlier, after the wet winter and spring.

## 10. Greenville, South Carolina

Greenville stayed near the top, moving from 9th to 10th. Its pollen and allergy medicine use both scored worse than average, with an average supply of allergists.

## Should you move to escape allergies?

The [AAFA](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/aafa-2026-allergy-capitals-report.pdf) says probably not. Pollen travels hundreds of miles, and ragweed keeps spreading north. Even if you leave your current pollen behind, you could develop allergies to the plants in your new city. The AAFA also points out that you could live in its 100th ranked city and still have significant allergies.

What helps more is knowing your own triggers and timing. These steps come from the AAFA's 2026 report:

1. **Check the daily pollen count during your season.** On high days, stay inside when you can, and wear sunglasses and a hat when you go out.
2. **Keep windows closed during pollen season.** Afternoons are usually the peak pollen times.
3. **Use central air or an air cleaner with a HEPA filter.** The AAFA recommends either one if you can, to filter pollen out of the air you breathe indoors.
4. **Shower before bed.** It removes pollen from your body and keeps it out of your bedding.
5. **Start allergy medicine a couple of weeks before your season begins.** Nasal steroid sprays are the most effective type for nose symptoms, and many are sold over the counter.

Tree, grass and weed pollen can cause the same sneezing and itchy eyes, and allergy testing can help show which one you react to. Our guide to [allergy skin tests vs. blood tests](/blog/allergy-skin-test-vs-blood-test) explains the difference.

## Longer term options for pollen allergies

Allergy medicine controls symptoms while you take it. If it doesn't give you complete relief, the AAFA suggests talking with an allergist about immunotherapy, which can change the body's immune response to allergens.

**Allergy shots (SCIT)** take the longest. The [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/allergy-shots-%28immunotherapy%29) describes shots once or twice a week for four to six months during build-up, then every two to four weeks for three to five years.

**Allergy tablets and drops (SLIT)** go under the tongue. Our [comparison of allergy shots, allergy drops and ILIT](/learn-about-ilit/allergy-shots-vs-allergy-drops-vs-ilit) covers which allergens each one treats.

**ILIT (intralymphatic immunotherapy)** is the shortest course, and for some people a faster alternative to allergy shots. An allergist uses ultrasound to inject FDA approved allergen extract into a lymph node in the groin (just below the waist, near the hip). Giving the extract that way is an off label use. A typical course is about three injections over about two months, the schedule used in a [2008 trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2582048/) in people with grass pollen allergy. In that study, ILIT raised nasal tolerance to grass pollen within four months, and after three years the effect wasn't significantly different from three years of allergy shots, with fewer side effects. ILIT research is promising but smaller than the research behind allergy shots, and results vary. The [ILIT FDA FAQ](/learn-about-ilit#faq-fda) explains the off label question.
