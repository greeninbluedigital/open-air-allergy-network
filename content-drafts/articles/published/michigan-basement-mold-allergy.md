KEY TAKEAWAYS
- Symptoms that get worse in a damp basement may point to a mold allergy, and mold can also set off asthma attacks in people with asthma who are allergic to it.
- Keep basement humidity between 30% and 50%. A hygrometer tells you where you stand, and a dehumidifier helps keep it there.
- Fix the water first. Gutters, downspout extensions and grading can solve a great number of basement water problems, while a dehumidifier isn't a permanent fix.
- A moldy patch smaller than about 10 square feet is usually a do-it-yourself job, but Michigan's health department suggests hiring help if you have asthma or other breathing problems.
- You usually don't need a mold test. If you can see or smell mold, clean it up and fix the moisture.

FAQS
Q: Can mold in your basement make you sick?
A: It can make some people sick. The [Michigan Department of Health and Human Services](https://www.michigan.gov/mdhhs/safety-injury-prev/environmental-health/topics/mold) (MDHHS) lists a runny or stuffy nose, sneezing, itchy eyes, coughing, a sore throat, a rash and asthma attacks among the symptoms mold can cause, and says it can make breathing problems like asthma worse. The [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home) says allergic reactions to mold are common and can start right away or later.

Q: Can mold in the basement affect upstairs?
A: It can. The [University of Minnesota Extension](https://extension.umn.edu/moisture-and-mold-indoors/moisture-basements-causes-and-solutions) says most basements in Minnesota are connected to the rest of the house through ductwork or other openings, so moisture problems there can lead to health problems. If you think mold has gotten into your heating and cooling system, the [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home) says not to run it, because it could spread mold through the house.

Q: What should I set my dehumidifier to in the basement?
A: Aim for 50% or a little lower. The [AAFA](https://aafa.org/allergies/types-of-allergies/mold-allergy/) says mold thrives above 50% humidity and suggests using a dehumidifier to keep your home below that, with a goal of 30% to 50%. It suggests checking with a hygrometer and draining the dehumidifier often.

Q: Will a dehumidifier help with mold in the basement?
A: It helps by keeping the air dry, but it won't fix the cause. The [University of Minnesota Extension](https://extension.umn.edu/moisture-and-mold-indoors/moisture-basements-causes-and-solutions) says a dehumidifier can reduce the humidity and odor in a basement but isn't a permanent or complete solution to a moisture problem. Mold that's already growing still has to be cleaned up, and the [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home) notes that even dead mold may still cause allergic reactions in some people.

Q: How do you test your basement for mold?
A: In most homes you don't need to. If mold is growing, you'll see it or smell a musty odor, [MDHHS](https://www.michigan.gov/mdhhs/safety-injury-prev/environmental-health/topics/mold) says, and the department doesn't recommend testing your home. The [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home) agrees that sampling is usually unnecessary when you can see the mold, since no federal limits exist to compare results against.

Q: Why do I sneeze in my basement?
A: Mold is a likely suspect. The [AAFA](https://aafa.org/allergies/types-of-allergies/mold-allergy/) says symptoms that often get worse in a damp, humid or moldy room like a basement may mean you have a mold allergy. Mold can also irritate the eyes, nose and throat of people who aren't allergic, according to the [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home), so it takes an allergist's skin or blood test, which the AAFA says doctors often use, to show whether you're allergic.

Q: Do air purifiers help with mold allergies?
A: Only once the humidity is under control. The [AAFA](https://aafa.org/allergies/types-of-allergies/mold-allergy/) says air cleaners and filters can't reduce mold spores in a home that's too humid. It suggests central air with a Certified Asthma & Allergy Friendly filter to trap spores through the whole house, since a freestanding air cleaner only filters a limited area.

Q: What is the best treatment for mold allergies?
A: Avoiding mold comes first, then medicine for symptoms: the [AAFA](https://aafa.org/allergies/types-of-allergies/mold-allergy/) names antihistamines and nasal corticosteroid sprays, both sold without a prescription. To treat the allergy itself, [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/22309-mold-allergy) says an allergist may suggest allergy shots, which may take several months to start working. Intralymphatic immunotherapy is a much shorter course of injections, but its key trial was in grass pollen allergy, so ask an allergist whether your mold results fit it, and use [Find a Provider](/find-an-ilit-provider) on OpenAirAllergyNetwork.com to locate an allergist who offers it.

For many Michigan homeowners, basement mold allergy shows up as a stuffy nose, sneezing or itchy eyes that get worse every time they go downstairs to do laundry, and it starts with water: mold can't grow without moisture, so a damp basement gives it a place to grow. Breathing in or touching mold spores may cause allergic reactions in sensitive people, and mold can cause asthma attacks in people with asthma who are allergic to it, according to the [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home).

Whether your basement takes on water after a storm or just smells musty all summer, the fix runs in the same order: find the moisture, keep humidity down and clean up what's already growing. If your symptoms keep coming back, treatments that go further than symptom relief range from allergy shots and allergy drops to ILIT (intralymphatic immunotherapy), a faster alternative to allergy shots.

## When to get care right away

Mold can set off asthma attacks in people with asthma who are allergic to it, the [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home) says. If you have severe trouble breathing, call 911 or go to an emergency room, [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/22309-mold-allergy) advises. Our guide to [allergies and chest tightness](/blog/can-allergies-cause-chest-tightness) explains allergic asthma.

## Basement mold allergy symptoms

The [Asthma and Allergy Foundation of America](https://aafa.org/allergies/types-of-allergies/mold-allergy/) (AAFA) says symptoms that often get worse in a damp, humid or moldy room like a basement may mean you have a mold allergy. The [Michigan Department of Health and Human Services](https://www.michigan.gov/mdhhs/safety-injury-prev/environmental-health/topics/mold) (MDHHS) lists a runny or stuffy nose, sneezing, itchy eyes, coughing and a sore throat among the symptoms mold can cause, along with a rash and trouble breathing. For people with asthma, it says, mold can make symptoms worse.

The [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home) adds that allergic reactions to mold are common and can be immediate or delayed, and that touching mold, not just breathing it in, may cause a reaction. Mold is also an irritant, so it can bother the eyes, nose, throat and lungs of people who aren't allergic.

The MDHHS fact sheets suggest noticing how you feel away from the place you suspect. If you feel better after leaving it for a while, it may have mold. If your symptoms don't go away, you might have a cold or another illness instead.

## Why Michigan basements get damp

Water reaches a basement three ways, the [University of Minnesota Extension](https://extension.umn.edu/moisture-and-mold-indoors/moisture-basements-causes-and-solutions) explains: rain and groundwater, moisture made inside the house, and humid outdoor air that condenses on cool basement walls. A 1-inch rain drops 1,250 gallons on the roof of a 2,000 square foot house, and without good gutters and grading, some of it flows into the basement. The water table can also rise with flooding or seasonal conditions.

Heavy storms are also getting heavier: researchers at [GLISA](https://glisa.umich.edu/resources-tools/climate-impacts/extreme-precipitation/), a University of Michigan climate center, report that the amount of precipitation falling in the heaviest 1% of storms rose 42% in the Midwest from 1958 through 2016. [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/22309-mold-allergy) suggests keeping an eye on basement walls and floors, especially after heavy rains.

In summer, open basement windows let in warm, humid air that condenses on the cool walls and floor. The University of Minnesota says people often mistake that condensation for a leak.

## Signs of mold in your basement

If mold is growing, you'll be able to see it or smell it, [MDHHS](https://www.michigan.gov/mdhhs/safety-injury-prev/environmental-health/topics/mold) says. It can be white, gray, brown, black, yellow, orange or green, and it smells musty, like old books or wet dirt. A moldy smell with no visible source may mean the mold is hidden, the [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home) says, in places such as the back of drywall or paneling and the underside of carpet and pads.

Among the signs of a basement moisture problem, the University of Minnesota lists a ring of dampness at the base of block walls, damp or humid air, condensation on walls and floors in summer, and standing water. Condensation on windows, walls or pipes can be a sign of high humidity, the EPA says.

## Basement humidity level: what to aim for

Keep your basement humidity between 30% and 50%. The [AAFA](https://aafa.org/allergies/types-of-allergies/mold-allergy/) says mold thrives above 50%, and the EPA puts the ceiling at 60%, with 30% to 50% as ideal. A hygrometer, a small humidity meter sold at hardware stores, tells you where your basement stands.

When you choose a dehumidifier, [ENERGY STAR](https://www.energystar.gov/products/dehumidifiers) says it's better to oversize than undersize. If your basement usually stays below 65°F, it suggests considering a model made for lower temperatures, since frost can form on the coils and keep the unit from removing moisture.

## How to keep basement mold down

These steps come from the EPA, the AAFA and the University of Minnesota Extension.

1. **Move rainwater away from the house.** The [University of Minnesota](https://extension.umn.edu/moisture-and-mold-indoors/moisture-basements-causes-and-solutions) says handling rainwater with gutters, downspouts and grading can solve a great number of basement water problems. Downspout extensions should drop water at least 4 feet from the wall, and the ground should slope away at least 1 inch per foot for 6 feet. The AAFA adds clearing leaves and dead plants from the foundation and gutters.
2. **Cover the sump pit.** An open sump pit can draw moist air into the basement, so the University of Minnesota recommends an airtight cover.
3. **Run a dehumidifier.** [MDHHS](https://www.michigan.gov/mdhhs/safety-injury-prev/environmental-health/topics/mold) recommends one for a damp basement or basement apartment. Drain it often and clean the coils and bucket, the AAFA says, because dehumidifiers can grow mold too. A hose to a floor drain or sump saves emptying the bucket, ENERGY STAR notes. A dehumidifier treats the dampness and the odor but not water that's getting in, so keep working on the cause.
4. **Keep basement windows closed on humid days.** Warm summer air condenses on the cool walls, and the [University of Minnesota Extension](https://extension.umn.edu/moisture-and-mold-indoors/dealing-and-preventing-mold-your-home) says not to keep windows open when it's damp outside while you run a dehumidifier or air conditioner.
5. **Swap basement carpet for hard flooring.** Carpet can hold onto moisture, so both the AAFA and the [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/conditions-treatments/allergies/mold-allergy/) suggest removing it. Instead of wall-to-wall carpet on a concrete floor, the University of Minnesota suggests painting the concrete and using area rugs you can take outside to clean and dry.
6. **Dry floods within 24 to 48 hours.** Wet materials dried in that window usually won't grow mold, the [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home) says. MDHHS says carpet and couches that stayed wet for two days or more will probably need to go.

If you're planning to finish a basement, dry it out first. The University of Minnesota warns that finishing a damp basement can make health problems worse.

## How to get rid of mold in a basement safely

A moldy patch smaller than about 10 square feet, roughly 3 feet by 3 feet, is usually a job you can handle yourself, according to the [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home). Scrub hard surfaces with detergent and water and let them dry completely. Porous things like carpet and ceiling tiles may have to be thrown away. Don't paint or caulk over mold, because the paint is likely to peel.

While you work, the EPA suggests an N-95 respirator, long gloves and goggles without vent holes. Bleach isn't recommended for routine cleanup, and it should never be mixed with ammonia. If anyone in your home has asthma or other breathing problems, MDHHS says not to use bleach at all, and the AAFA warns that bleach may cause asthma symptoms, so wear a mask or have someone else do the cleaning if possible.

For a larger area, or if you have asthma or other breathing problems, [MDHHS](https://www.michigan.gov/mdhhs/safety-injury-prev/environmental-health/topics/mold) suggests hiring a contractor. Michigan doesn't license or certify companies for mold cleanup, it notes, and an ordinary home repair contractor can do the work. Dead mold may still cause allergic reactions, the EPA says, so killing it isn't enough: it has to be removed. If a broken pipe caused the damage, MDHHS points out that homeowner's insurance may cover it, and there's a chance the policy also covers the water damage repairs.

## Testing: the house or you?

You usually don't need to test your basement. MDHHS doesn't recommend mold testing and can't test homes, and the EPA says sampling is usually unnecessary when you can see mold, since no federal limits exist to compare results against. Clean it up and fix the moisture instead.

For your own symptoms, an allergist can confirm a mold allergy from your history, an exam and a skin or blood test, the AAFA says. Our guides to [skin tests vs. blood tests](/blog/allergy-skin-test-vs-blood-test) and [reading your allergy test results](/blog/allergy-test-results) explain what to expect, and the [Michigan allergy season guide](/blog/michigan-allergy-season) shows when outdoor mold and pollen add to the load.

## Longer term options for mold allergy

For symptoms, the [AAFA](https://aafa.org/allergies/types-of-allergies/mold-allergy/) says antihistamines and nasal corticosteroid sprays are sold without a prescription.

**Allergy shots (SCIT)** treat the allergy itself. A [2024 review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38685477/) in the AAAAI's practice journal says studies have shown allergen immunotherapy works for allergic rhinitis and asthma caused by certain mold spores. The [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/allergy-shots-%28immunotherapy%29) describes shots once or twice a week for four to six months, then every two to four weeks for three to five years.

**Allergy tablets and drops (SLIT)** go under the tongue. The same review lists the U.S. approved tablets as grass, ragweed, dust mite, birch-type tree and Japanese cedar, so there's no FDA approved mold tablet.

**ILIT (intralymphatic immunotherapy)** is a much shorter course and a fast allergy treatment option for some people. An allergist uses ultrasound to inject FDA approved allergen extract into a lymph node, usually in the groin (just below the waist, near the hip). Giving extract that way is an off label use, as our [FDA FAQ](/learn-about-ilit#faq-fda) explains. A typical course is about three injections over about two months, the schedule from a [2008 trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2582048/) in people with grass pollen allergy, where results after three years were comparable to three years of allergy shots, with fewer allergic side effects. That trial studied grass pollen, and ILIT research is smaller than the research behind allergy shots, so ask an allergist whether your mold results fit a course.

Our [comparison of allergy shots, allergy drops and ILIT](/learn-about-ilit/allergy-shots-vs-allergy-drops-vs-ilit) covers the details.
