KEY TAKEAWAYS
- Mold allergy symptoms look like hay fever: sneezing, a runny or stuffy nose, and itchy, watery eyes. In people with asthma, mold can also set off wheezing and chest tightness.
- Outdoor mold symptoms are most common from July to the first hard frost, but indoor mold can cause them all year.
- Mold allergy isn't usually behind ongoing headaches or fatigue, according to the AAAAI.
- Keep indoor humidity between 30% and 50%, fix leaks quickly, and let someone else rake the leaves.
- Allergy shots can treat allergies to certain molds, and they're the main immunotherapy for mold, since there's no FDA approved mold allergy tablet.

FAQS
Q: Can a mold allergy cause a skin rash?
A: It can. The [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/mold/mold-and-health) lists skin rash among the allergic responses to mold, along with sneezing, a runny nose and red eyes, and says touching mold or its spores, not only breathing them in, may set off a reaction. The [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/mold-health/about/index.html) also lists skin rash among the symptoms mold can cause in some people.

Q: Can mold allergy cause headaches?
A: Not usually as an ongoing symptom. The [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/conditions-treatments/allergies/mold-allergy/) says mold allergy can trigger nose, eye and asthma symptoms but is not usually a cause of chronic symptoms such as headache and fatigue.

Q: When does mold allergy season end?
A: Outdoors, it usually winds down in winter. The [AAFA](https://aafa.org/allergies/types-of-allergies/mold-allergy/) says symptoms from outdoor mold are most common from July until the first hard frost, and that unlike pollen, mold doesn't die with the frost. Most outdoor molds go inactive over winter, then grow again in spring on plants the cold killed. Indoor mold can keep causing symptoms year round, and the [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/outdoor-allergens-ttr) says outdoor mold can be found all year in the South and on the West Coast.

Q: Can mold allergy cause a fever?
A: Fever isn't a typical mold allergy symptom. The [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/mold-health/about/index.html) says severe reactions such as fever or shortness of breath may occur in workers exposed to large amounts of mold on the job, such as farmers working around moldy hay. Our guide to [allergies vs. a cold](/blog/allergies-or-a-cold) explains how allergy symptoms differ from an infection's.

Q: Is a mold allergy the same as mold toxicity?
A: No. A mold allergy is an immune reaction to mold spores, and an allergist can confirm it with a skin or blood test. The [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/toxic-mold) says the Institute of Medicine and the World Health Organization both found the evidence insufficient to link breathing in mold toxins to the broad symptoms described as "toxic mold syndrome", and it warns that urine mycotoxin tests sold to consumers aren't FDA approved or standardized. [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/24862-black-mold) says a black mold allergy isn't unique or more harmful than other mold allergies.

Q: How do you know if you are allergic to mold?
A: See an allergist. The [AAFA](https://aafa.org/allergies/types-of-allergies/mold-allergy/) says doctors diagnose mold allergy from your medical history, a physical exam and a skin or blood test, and it notes that symptoms that often get worse in a damp, humid or moldy room like a basement may point to mold. The [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/mold-health/about/index.html) doesn't recommend testing your home for mold, since sampling can't tell you whether you'll get sick.

Q: What foods should you avoid with a mold allergy?
A: Not necessarily. A mold allergy is mostly a reaction to spores you breathe in, and the University of Nebraska's [Food Allergy Research and Resource Program](https://farrp.unl.edu/farrp-resources/general-information-food-allergies-sensitivities/opinions-and-summaries/mold/) says no evidence shows mold ripened cheeses harm people with mold allergy, though some doctors still advise caution.

Q: Do mold allergy shots work?
A: For certain molds, yes. A [2024 review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38685477/) in the AAAAI's practice journal says studies have shown allergy shots work for allergic rhinitis and asthma caused by certain mold spores, and the [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/conditions-treatments/allergies/mold-allergy/) says shots can give long-term relief. Intralymphatic immunotherapy is a shorter course of injections, so ask an allergist whether your mold results fit it, and use [Find a Provider](/find-an-ilit-provider) on OpenAirAllergyNetwork.com to locate an allergist who offers it.

A mold allergy happens when your immune system overreacts to the tiny spores that molds release into the air, and the symptoms look a lot like hay fever: sneezing, a runny or stuffy nose, and itchy, watery eyes. Outdoors, mold allergy symptoms are most common from July until the first hard frost, according to the [Asthma and Allergy Foundation of America](https://aafa.org/allergies/types-of-allergies/mold-allergy/) (AAFA), but mold growing indoors can cause them in any month.

Whether the mold that bothers you lives in a pile of wet leaves or under the bathroom sink, the first steps are the same: confirm that mold is the trigger, then cut down how much of it you breathe. Below are the symptoms, the season, testing, and the treatments that go further than symptom relief, from allergy shots and allergy drops to ILIT (intralymphatic immunotherapy), a faster alternative to allergy shots.

## When to get care right away

Mold can set off asthma attacks in people with asthma who are allergic to it, the [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/mold/mold-and-health) says. If you have severe trouble breathing, call 911 or go to an emergency room, as [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/22309-mold-allergy) advises. Our guide to [allergies and chest tightness](/blog/can-allergies-cause-chest-tightness) explains allergic asthma.

## What is a mold allergy?

Molds are fungi, and they can grow on almost anything that stays damp, indoors or out. The [American College of Allergy, Asthma and Immunology](https://acaai.org/allergies/allergic-conditions/mold-allergies/) (ACAAI) counts roughly 1,000 species of mold in the U.S. The [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/conditions-treatments/allergies/mold-allergy/) says everyone breathes in mold spores, but in people with a mold allergy the immune system treats certain spores as a threat. The molds that most often cause allergy symptoms are *Alternaria*, *Aspergillus*, *Cladosporium* and *Penicillium*, according to both the AAAAI and the AAFA.

[Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/22309-mold-allergy) cites one study estimating that 3 to 10 of every 100 people have it, and it says a family history of allergies or a lung condition like asthma raises your risk.

## Mold allergy symptoms

Mold allergy symptoms are mostly in the nose and eyes. The AAFA lists:

- Runny nose, usually clear and thin
- Stuffy nose
- Postnasal drip
- Sneezing
- Itchy nose, eyes, ears and mouth
- Red, watery eyes
- Swelling around the eyes

Cleveland Clinic adds coughing, an itchy throat and wheezing, and the [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/mold-health/about/index.html) says mold can also cause a skin rash in some people. Symptoms can start right away or show up later, the AAFA notes.

If you're allergic to mold and also have asthma, mold can trigger wheezing, shortness of breath and chest tightness. Rarely, the AAFA says, mold causes a more serious lung condition called allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis, with symptoms much like asthma. Mold allergy isn't usually a cause of ongoing symptoms such as headache and fatigue, the [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/conditions-treatments/allergies/mold-allergy/) says.

Symptoms that often get worse in a damp, humid or moldy room, such as a basement, may point to a mold allergy, the AAFA says. Mold is also an irritant, the AAFA explains, so spores can bother your eyes, nose and lungs even if you aren't allergic.

## Mold allergy season: when outdoor mold peaks

Outdoor mold spores start to rise as temperatures warm in spring. In the U.S., the [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/outdoor-allergens-ttr) says they peak in July in warmer states and in October in colder ones, and they can be found year round in the South and on the West Coast. The AAFA puts the most common months for symptoms at July through the first hard frost. Indoors, mold grows in damp spots like bathrooms, kitchens and basements, so it can keep symptoms going in any month.

### Leaf mold allergy in fall

Many molds grow on fallen leaves, rotting logs, compost piles, grasses and grains, the AAFA says. Raking and mowing stir those spores up, the [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/outdoor-allergens-ttr) notes. Unlike pollen, mold doesn't die with the first frost. Most outdoor molds go quiet over winter and start growing again in spring on plants the cold killed.

## How to know if you're allergic to mold

Mold allergy symptoms often mimic other conditions, the [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/conditions-treatments/allergies/mold-allergy/) says, and an allergist can tell whether mold is behind yours. A skin prick test gives results in a few minutes. A blood test that looks for IgE antibodies to molds is the other option, [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/22309-mold-allergy) explains. Our guides to [skin tests vs. blood tests](/blog/allergy-skin-test-vs-blood-test) and [reading your allergy test results](/blog/allergy-test-results) cover what to expect.

Tracking your symptoms for two weeks, along with where you've been, may help you find the source, the [ACAAI](https://acaai.org/allergies/allergic-conditions/mold-allergies/) says.

Testing your house for mold is another story: the [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/mold-health/about/index.html) doesn't recommend mold sampling, because it can't tell you whether you'll get sick. Instead, the CDC advises removing mold promptly, whatever the type, the [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/toxic-mold) notes.

## How to cut down your mold exposure

These steps come from the [AAFA](https://aafa.org/allergies/types-of-allergies/mold-allergy/) and the AAAAI.

1. **Keep indoor humidity between 30% and 50%.** Mold thrives above 50%, the AAFA says. A hygrometer tells you where your home stands, and an air conditioner or dehumidifier helps keep it low, the [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/mold-health/about/index.html) says. Drain the dehumidifier often and clean its coils and bucket, since it can grow mold of its own. Basements are a common trouble spot, and our [basement mold guide](/blog/michigan-basement-mold-allergy) covers humidity, drainage and cleanup.
2. **Fix leaks fast.** Plumbing, roof and gutter leaks all feed mold. If your home floods, it needs to be dry within 24 to 48 hours to prevent mold, according to the AAFA and the [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/mold/mold-and-health).
3. **Clean small patches with detergent and water.** Scrub hard surfaces and let them dry fully. Wear goggles, gloves and a mask, and throw away soft items like carpet if the mold won't come out. If you use bleach, the AAFA warns it may set off asthma symptoms, so ventilate well and wear a mask or have someone else do it.
4. **Let someone else rake.** The AAAAI says to stay away from uncut fields and avoid raking leaves. If you have to do yard work, the ACAAI suggests wearing a dust mask or taking allergy medicine beforehand, then rinsing your nose with saline and showering afterward.
5. **Stay in on high mold count days.** The AAFA and AAAAI both suggest limiting time outdoors when [mold counts](/blog/how-to-read-a-pollen-count) are high. A shower when you come in washes spores out of your hair before bed.

## Mold allergy treatment and medicine

For the symptoms themselves, the AAFA says antihistamines and nasal corticosteroid sprays are sold without a prescription, and the [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/conditions-treatments/allergies/mold-allergy/) adds eye drops and other nasal sprays.

There's no cure for a mold allergy, [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/22309-mold-allergy) says, but the AAFA notes that immunotherapy may cut both symptoms and the medicine you need.

## Longer term options for mold allergy

**Allergy shots (SCIT)** are the main immunotherapy for mold. A [2024 review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38685477/) in the AAAAI's practice journal says studies have shown shots work for allergic rhinitis and asthma caused by certain mold spores. Extracts for *Alternaria*, one of the most common allergy molds, are hard to standardize, and a [2024 review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38865977/) says that hampers how well and how safely those shots work. The [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/allergy-shots-%28immunotherapy%29) describes the usual schedule as shots once or twice a week for four to six months, then every two to four weeks for three to five years.

**Allergy tablets and drops (SLIT)** go under the tongue. The same 2024 review lists the tablets regulators have approved: grass, ragweed, dust mite, birch-type tree and Japanese cedar. None is for mold. Liquid drops are used off label.

**ILIT (intralymphatic immunotherapy)** is a much shorter course and a fast allergy treatment option for some people. An allergist uses ultrasound to inject FDA approved allergen extract into a lymph node, usually in the groin (just below the waist, near the hip). Giving extract that way is an off label use, as our [FDA FAQ](/learn-about-ilit#faq-fda) explains. A typical course is about three injections over about two months, the schedule from a [2008 trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2582048/) in people with grass pollen allergy, where results after three years were comparable to three years of allergy shots, with fewer allergic side effects. ILIT research is promising but smaller than the research behind allergy shots, and the trial behind the schedule studied grass pollen, so ask an allergist whether your mold results fit a course.
