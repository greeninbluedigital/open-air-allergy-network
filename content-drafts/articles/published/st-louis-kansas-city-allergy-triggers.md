FAQS
Q: Is Kansas City bad for allergies?
A: Kansas City rates about average nationally, though its 2026 rank was tougher than the year before. The Asthma and Allergy Foundation of America ranked Kansas City 42nd of 100 metro areas in its [2026 Allergy Capitals report](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/aafa-2026-allergy-capitals-report.pdf), up from 54th in [2025](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/aafa-2025-allergy-capitals-report.pdf). Its pollen scored average, but people there used more over-the-counter allergy medicine than average. If over-the-counter medicine isn't keeping up, an allergist can test for the pollens behind your symptoms.

Q: Is St. Louis bad for allergies?
A: St. Louis ranks lower than most big metros, but that doesn't mean its allergy seasons are mild. The [AAFA's 2026 Allergy Capitals report](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/aafa-2026-allergy-capitals-report.pdf) put St. Louis 63rd of 100 metro areas, with pollen scores better than average and more allergists than average, yet people there used more over-the-counter allergy medicine than average. The AAFA adds that you could live in the 100th city and still have significant allergies, so judge by your own symptoms, not the rank.

Q: Why are allergies so bad in Missouri?
A: Missouri's pollen season runs most of the year, and counts appear to be rising. Dr. Christine Franzese of the University of Missouri told [KBIA](https://www.kbia.org/kbia-news/2026-04-30/achoo-mu-researchers-show-pollen-counts-rising-over-time) in 2026 that the allergy season most people feel lasts about nine months, and that counts at the St. Louis pollen station seem to be getting higher year to year. She suggests allergy testing so you know which tree, grass or weed to track.

Q: Why are allergies so bad in Kansas?
A: Kansas has one of the country's toughest allergy cities. Wichita ranked first in the AAFA's Allergy Capitals report for three years in a row through [2025](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/aafa-2025-allergy-capitals-report.pdf) and was still 6th in [2026](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/aafa-2026-allergy-capitals-report.pdf). The AAFA says ragweed populations are particularly high in Midwestern states, and [KCUR](https://www.kcur.org/2023-12-07/eastern-redcedar-spread-great-plains) reports eastern red cedars spreading across grasslands in Kansas and other Great Plains states.

Q: When is allergy season in Missouri?
A: Allergy season in Missouri usually runs from February to the first hard frost. The [St. Louis County Pollen and Mold Center](https://pollenandmold.stlouisco.com/html/FAQ.html) says trees typically pollinate from February to May, grasses from May to mid July, and weeds peak from late summer to early fall. Around Kansas City, [Children's Mercy](https://www.childrensmercy.org/parent-ish/2026/04/seasonal-allergies/) puts trees at February through May, grass in May and June and weeds starting in mid to late August. Mark the months your symptoms start, since that pattern points to your trigger.

Q: When is allergy season over in Missouri?
A: Pollen season usually ends with the first hard frost, but mold keeps going. The [St. Louis County Pollen and Mold Center](https://pollenandmold.stlouisco.com/html/FAQ.html) says weeds grow from late summer until the first hard frost, and the [Missouri Department of Health and Senior Services](https://health.mo.gov/data-dashboards-tools/environmental-public-health-tracking/epht-pollen-and-mold) says outdoor mold counts are especially high in the fall. Symptoms that last through winter usually point indoors: the [ACAAI](https://acaai.org/news/do-you-have-the-winter-sniffles-5-ways-you-can-manage-indoor-winter-allergies/) names dust mites, pet allergens and indoor mold, which are there all year but bother people more in winter, when the house is shut and the heat recirculates the air.

Q: Is there cedar fever in Missouri?
A: "Cedar fever" is the Texas name for mountain cedar (Ashe juniper) allergy, but Missouri has cedar pollen of its own. The [Missouri Department of Conservation](https://mdc.mo.gov/discover-nature/field-guide/eastern-red-cedar) says the state's eastern red cedar, a juniper, sheds pollen from March to May, and the [Missouri Botanical Garden](https://www.missouribotanicalgarden.org/gardens-gardening/your-garden/help-for-the-home-gardener/advice-tips-resources/visual-guides/plants-pollen-and-allergies.aspx) says junipers can make large amounts of highly allergenic pollen. Our [cedar fever guide](/blog/cedar-fever-mountain-cedar-allergy) covers the Texas version.

Q: Where can I find the Kansas City and St. Louis pollen counts?
A: Both cities have their own counting programs. In Kansas City, [Children's Mercy](https://childrensmercy.org/pollen) posts a daily pollen and mold count with the top pollens and molds during its counting season. In St. Louis, the [St. Louis County Pollen and Mold Center](https://pollenandmold.stlouisco.com/) updates its report every business day by 11 a.m. and has published counts since 1960. Our guide to [reading a pollen count](/blog/how-to-read-a-pollen-count) explains what the levels mean.

Q: Is there a faster alternative to allergy shots in Kansas City or St. Louis?
A: Yes. ILIT (intralymphatic immunotherapy) is typically about three injections over about two months, while the [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/allergy-shots-%28immunotherapy%29) describes allergy shots continuing for three to five years once you reach maintenance. It's given into a lymph node in the groin (just below the waist, near the hip), and its research base is smaller than that of allergy shots. To see whether it fits your allergies, use [Find a Provider](/find-an-ilit-provider) on OpenAirAllergyNetwork.com to locate an allergist who offers it.

Kansas City allergies and St. Louis allergies follow much the same calendar across Missouri and Kansas, starting with tree pollen such as oak and cedar in late winter and spring and ending with ragweed and outdoor mold in the fall. The Asthma and Allergy Foundation of America (AAFA) ranked Kansas City 42nd and St. Louis 63rd of the 100 largest metro areas in the contiguous U.S. in its [2026 Allergy Capitals report](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/aafa-2026-allergy-capitals-report.pdf). Kansas City's pollen scored average and St. Louis's scored better than average, but people in both cities used more over-the-counter allergy medicine than average.

Whichever of these triggers gets to you, allergy medicine treats the symptoms while you take it. For longer relief, allergy shots, allergy drops and ILIT, a fast allergy treatment course of typically three injections, work on the allergy itself.

## Common allergy triggers in Kansas City and St. Louis

Missouri health and plant sources point to five seasonal allergy triggers in Kansas City and St. Louis: eastern red cedar, spring trees such as oak and hickory, grass, ragweed and outdoor mold. The plants come from University of Missouri Health Care's list of the state's biggest tree triggers, the [Missouri Botanical Garden](https://www.missouribotanicalgarden.org/gardens-gardening/your-garden/help-for-the-home-gardener/advice-tips-resources/visual-guides/plants-pollen-and-allergies.aspx) and the Missouri Department of Conservation. Most of the timing comes from the two local counting programs, at Children's Mercy Kansas City and the St. Louis County Pollen and Mold Center. No source ranks these triggers by how many people they bother, so the list runs in calendar order.

| Trigger | When it's worst | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Eastern red cedar | March to May | A juniper, and junipers can make highly allergenic pollen |
| Oak, hickory, walnut and ash | Early spring (tree season runs February to May) | Among Missouri's biggest tree pollen triggers |
| Grass | May and June in Kansas City, May to mid July in St. Louis | Light pollen that travels on the wind |

## 1. Eastern red cedar allergy in Missouri and Kansas

Eastern red cedar is a juniper (Juniperus virginiana), and the [Missouri Department of Conservation](https://mdc.mo.gov/discover-nature/field-guide/eastern-red-cedar) (MDC) says it sheds pollen from March to May. The [Missouri Botanical Garden](https://www.missouribotanicalgarden.org/gardens-gardening/your-garden/help-for-the-home-gardener/advice-tips-resources/visual-guides/plants-pollen-and-allergies.aspx) says junipers can produce large amounts of highly allergenic pollen.

MDC says red cedar was uncommon in Missouri before European settlement and grew mostly on bluffs and cliffs where fire couldn't reach it. With fire suppression and other changes in land use, it's now commonplace. Across the state line, [KCUR](https://www.kcur.org/2023-12-07/eastern-redcedar-spread-great-plains) reports red cedars taking over grasslands from Texas up through Oklahoma, Kansas and Nebraska.

**Tip:** Red cedars usually come as separate male and female trees, MDC says, and the Missouri Botanical Garden notes that female-only juniper cultivars don't make pollen. If you're planting junipers near the house, ask the nursery for a female-only cultivar.

## 2. Spring tree pollen: oak, hickory, walnut and ash

Missouri's biggest tree pollen triggers are oak, cedar, hickory, walnut and ash, according to MU Health Care, as reported by [KBIA](https://www.kbia.org/kbia-news/2026-04-30/achoo-mu-researchers-show-pollen-counts-rising-over-time). The [Missouri Botanical Garden](https://www.missouribotanicalgarden.org/gardens-gardening/your-garden/help-for-the-home-gardener/advice-tips-resources/visual-guides/plants-pollen-and-allergies.aspx) calls oaks very common in Missouri and says they release large amounts of pollen in early spring. It singles out shagbark hickory as widespread in the state and a bad allergen.

[Children's Mercy](https://www.childrensmercy.org/parent-ish/2026/04/seasonal-allergies/) in Kansas City says trees begin pollinating in early to mid February, before they leaf out, and each type releases pollen at its own time between February and May. That's why spring symptoms can drag on or shift from week to week. The [St. Louis County Pollen and Mold Center](https://pollenandmold.stlouisco.com/html/FAQ.html) gives the same February to May window for St. Louis.

**Tip:** Keep windows closed at night and in the car during tree season, as St. Louis County advises. Our [tree pollen allergy guide](/blog/tree-pollen-allergy) covers symptoms and the foods that can cross react with tree pollen.

## 3. Grass pollen season in Kansas City and St. Louis

[Children's Mercy](https://www.childrensmercy.org/parent-ish/2026/04/seasonal-allergies/) puts Kansas City's grass season in May and June and calls it intense but shorter than tree season. In St. Louis, the [county's pollen center](https://pollenandmold.stlouisco.com/html/FAQ.html) says grasses run from May to mid July. The [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/mowing-down-your-grass-allergies) lists Timothy, Kentucky blue, Johnson, rye and fescue among the common northern grasses.

Your own lawn may matter less than you think: the [Missouri Botanical Garden](https://www.missouribotanicalgarden.org/gardens-gardening/your-garden/help-for-the-home-gardener/advice-tips-resources/visual-guides/plants-pollen-and-allergies.aspx) says lawns mowed regularly aren't a concern because they don't get to flower. Ornamental and wild grasses are the ones to avoid, and grasses usually shed plenty of pollen from early summer into fall, depending on the species. Grass pollen is light and can blow in from other places, the [AAFA](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/aafa-2026-allergy-capitals-report.pdf) says.

**Tip:** St. Louis County suggests staying away from fresh-cut grass during your season. If you have to mow, change and wash your clothes afterward, as the AAFA advises after outdoor activities.

## 4. Ragweed in Kansas City and St. Louis

[Children's Mercy](https://www.childrensmercy.org/parent-ish/2026/04/seasonal-allergies/) calls ragweed the most dominant weed allergen in the Kansas City area and says weed pollen shows up in mid to late August and peaks around Labor Day. The [AAFA](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/aafa-2026-allergy-capitals-report.pdf) says about 1 in 7 people are allergic to ragweed and that ragweed populations are particularly high in Midwestern states. A single plant can produce up to 1 billion pollen grains, according to the [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/ragweed).

Goldenrod blooms at the same time and often gets blamed, but the [Missouri Botanical Garden](https://www.missouribotanicalgarden.org/gardens-gardening/your-garden/help-for-the-home-gardener/advice-tips-resources/visual-guides/plants-pollen-and-allergies.aspx) says goldenrod is much less allergenic, with heavy pollen that doesn't travel far, and that ragweed is usually the culprit.

**Tip:** Start your allergy medicine a couple of weeks before mid August. The AAFA says starting before your season gives the best results. For ragweed symptoms and treatment, see our ragweed allergy guide below.

## 5. Outdoor mold in Missouri and Kansas

The [Missouri Department of Health and Senior Services](https://health.mo.gov/data-dashboards-tools/environmental-public-health-tracking/epht-pollen-and-mold) says outdoor mold grows on decaying plants and leaves all year, and that mold counts are especially high in the fall. On October 21, 2026, the last day of its counting season, [Children's Mercy](https://childrensmercy.org/pollen) reported a low pollen count of 2 and a moderate mold count of 8,909 spores per cubic meter of air, most of it Cladosporium. That's one day's reading, but it shows how mold can outlast pollen.

**Tip:** If you have a mold allergy, the state health department suggests avoiding piles of decaying leaves, hay and mulch, and grass cutting. Fall leaf raking is a good job to hand off. Our [mold allergy guide](/blog/mold-allergy) covers the rest.

## How to cut your allergy exposure in Kansas City and St. Louis

**1. Check the local pollen and mold count.** [Children's Mercy](https://childrensmercy.org/pollen) posts a daily Kansas City pollen and mold count during its season, and the [St. Louis County Pollen and Mold Center](https://pollenandmold.stlouisco.com/) updates its count every business day by 11 a.m. St. Louis County suggests staying inside on high count days and windy days. Our guide to [reading a pollen count](/blog/how-to-read-a-pollen-count) explains the levels.

**2. Keep pollen out of the house.** The [AAFA](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/aafa-2026-allergy-capitals-report.pdf) suggests taking your shoes off at the door, keeping windows closed during pollen season and running central air or a HEPA air cleaner if you can.

**3. Dry laundry inside.** St. Louis County says pollen and mold collect in laundry hung outdoors. Use the dryer or an indoor rack.

**4. Shower before bed.** The AAFA says it washes pollen off your body and keeps it out of your bedding.

**5. Use a nasal steroid spray.** The AAFA calls nasal corticosteroid sprays the most effective medicine type for nose allergy symptoms, and many are sold over the counter.

## Allergy testing in Kansas City and St. Louis

Cedar, oak, grass, ragweed and mold can all cause the same sneezing and itchy eyes, so the season alone won't always tell you which one you're reacting to. Dr. Christine Franzese of the University of Missouri recommends allergy testing, she told [KBIA](https://www.kbia.org/kbia-news/2026-04-30/achoo-mu-researchers-show-pollen-counts-rising-over-time) in 2026, so that people know exactly which tree, grass or weed to track. A skin test is read after about 15 minutes, according to the [ACAAI](https://acaai.org/allergies/allergic-conditions/ragweed-allergy/), so you usually leave the first visit knowing your results. Knowing your triggers is also the first step toward immunotherapy, which is matched to your allergens. Our guide to [allergy skin tests vs. blood tests](/blog/allergy-skin-test-vs-blood-test) explains both, and [Do You Need Allergy Testing Before ILIT?](/blog/allergy-testing-before-ilit) covers what happens at a first visit.

## Longer term options for Kansas City and St. Louis allergies

Allergy medicine eases symptoms while you take it, but it doesn't change the allergy. If medicine doesn't give you complete relief, the [AAFA](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/aafa-2026-allergy-capitals-report.pdf) suggests asking your allergist about immunotherapy, a long-term treatment that can change how your immune system responds to allergens.

**Allergy shots (SCIT)** follow the longest schedule. The [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/allergy-shots-%28immunotherapy%29) describes shots once or twice a week for four to six months during build-up, then every two to four weeks for three to five years. Our [guide to allergy shots](/learn-about-ilit/allergy-shots-and-allergy-immunotherapy) explains the schedule.

**Allergy tablets and drops (SLIT)** dissolve under the tongue. The AAAAI says tablets exist for [grass](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/mowing-down-your-grass-allergies) and [ragweed](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/ragweed), and grass tablets are taken at home after the first doses in the allergist's office. Our [comparison of allergy shots, allergy drops and ILIT](/learn-about-ilit/allergy-shots-vs-allergy-drops-vs-ilit) covers which allergens each one treats.

**ILIT (intralymphatic immunotherapy)** is the shortest course, and for some people a faster alternative to allergy shots. An allergist uses ultrasound to inject FDA-approved allergen extract into a lymph node in the groin (just below the waist, near the hip). Giving the extract that way is an off-label use. A typical course is about three injections over about two months, the schedule from a [2008 trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2582048/) in people with grass pollen allergy, which compared it with 54 allergy shots over three years. In that study, ILIT raised tolerance to grass pollen within four months, and after three years the effect wasn't significantly different from three years of allergy shots, with fewer allergic side effects.

ILIT research is promising but smaller than the research behind allergy shots, and results vary from person to person. The [ILIT FDA FAQ](/learn-about-ilit#faq-fda) covers the off-label question in more detail.
