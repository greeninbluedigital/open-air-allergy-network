- Bermuda grass can release pollen even when it's kept short, while perennial ryegrass and tall fescue usually don't flower until they're about 12 inches tall.

FAQS
Q: Is Los Angeles bad for allergies?
A: Not compared with most big U.S. metros. The [AAFA](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/aafa-2026-allergy-capitals-report.pdf) ranked Los Angeles 82nd of 100 in 2026, better than average overall and average for pollen. The ranking doesn't measure smoke, ash or mold, and the AAFA says you could live in its 100th city and still have significant allergies.

Q: What are the most common allergies in Los Angeles?
A: Pollen from grasses, trees and weeds is often the culprit behind seasonal allergies in Southern California, according to [Cedars-Sinai's pollen guide](https://www.cedars-sinai.org/content/dam/cedars-sinai/blog/2017/05/pollen-guide-in-southern-california.pdf). Its list of common sources includes Bermuda and rye grasses, olive, oak, walnut, western sycamore and eucalyptus trees, and weeds such as sagebrush, western ragweed and black mustard. An allergy test shows which ones you actually react to.

Q: When is peak allergy season in Los Angeles?
A: Late winter and spring. [UCLA Health](https://www.uclahealth.org/news/rain-allergies-kids-pollen) says tree pollen is most prevalent in Southern California from January through May. Heavy rain can make it worse: UCLA Health says storms can break pollen into smaller particles that are more easily inhaled and reach deeper into the lungs.

Q: When does allergy season end in Los Angeles?
A: For many people it never fully does. Weed pollen runs into early fall, a USC allergist [told LAist](https://laist.com/shows/take-two/socals-allergy-season-is-never-ending-and-could-get-worse), and the [AAFA](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/aafa-2026-allergy-capitals-report.pdf) says grass pollen can be found year-round in warmer parts of the country. Santa Ana winds, which can stir up irritants, blow from September through May, the [National Weather Service](https://www.weather.gov/safety/wind-mountain-valley) says.

Q: Do Santa Ana winds cause allergies?
A: Not directly, but they can make you feel like they do. During one Santa Ana event, UCLA allergist Dr. Rita Kachru [told LAist](https://laist.com/news/santa-ana-winds-allergies) that the pollen count was zero. LAist reported wildfire ash, other particles and mold spores as the likely culprits, and Dr. Kachru called the symptoms an inflammatory reaction to irritant particles rather than an allergy. She said ash from fires 90 to 100 miles away can be kicked up into the air, even if a fire was a year ago.

Q: Can wildfire smoke cause allergies?
A: Smoke is an irritant, not an allergen, so it doesn't create a new allergy, but it can make your nose, eyes and lungs feel worse. The [South Coast AQMD](https://www.aqmd.gov/home/air-quality/wildfire-health-info-smoke-tips) lists people with lung diseases such as asthma among those most vulnerable to smoke. It advises staying indoors with windows closed and wearing an N95 or P100 respirator, not a cloth or surgical mask, if you have to be outside.

Q: Where can I get allergy shots in Los Angeles?
A: At an allergist's office. The [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/allergy-shots-%28immunotherapy%29) says allergy shots are given in a health care setting such as an allergist's office, typically once or twice a week for four to six months and then every two to four weeks for three to five years. If you'd rather make far fewer trips, ILIT (intralymphatic immunotherapy) is a shorter course of about three injections over about two months. Use [Find a Provider](/find-an-ilit-provider) on OpenAirAllergyNetwork.com to locate an allergist who offers it.

Los Angeles allergies can last most of the year, because the Southern California allergy season keeps handing off from one trigger to the next. Tree pollen peaks from January through May, grass carries into summer and weeds take over into early fall, and then the Santa Ana winds arrive, kicking up ash and other irritants. The region's warm climate means there's pretty much always something sending pollen your way, as [LAist](https://laist.com/shows/take-two/socals-allergy-season-is-never-ending-and-could-get-worse) reported, and heavy rain can make the spring season worse, according to [UCLA Health](https://www.uclahealth.org/news/rain-allergies-kids-pollen).

Whichever of these allergy triggers get to you, there are two main ways to treat them: allergy pills and nasal sprays ease symptoms while you take them, and immunotherapy treats the allergy itself. Immunotherapy comes as allergy shots, allergy drops or tablets, or ILIT, a faster alternative to allergy shots that typically takes about three injections over about two months. Below, you'll find when each trigger is in the air in Los Angeles, how the city ranks for allergies, and how an allergist in Los Angeles can test you and match a treatment to what you're allergic to.

## Los Angeles allergy season at a glance

Southern California's allergy triggers fall into three pollen groups, plus the Santa Ana winds. The table shows the most common sources of each and when they're in the air.

| Trigger | Common sources in Southern California | When it's in the air |
|---|---|---|
| Tree pollen | Olive, oak, walnut, western sycamore, eucalyptus, elm, Arizona cypress | January through May |
| Grass pollen | Bermuda, ryegrass, fescue, timothy, orchard grass | Spring into summer, and can linger year-round |
| Weed pollen | Sagebrush, western ragweed, wormwood, amaranth, black mustard | Summer and early fall |
| Santa Ana winds (ash, fine particles, mold spores) | Dry desert winds blowing toward the coast | September through May, peaking in December and January |

*Santa Ana winds aren't an allergen. They carry irritants that can feel like allergies, covered below. Sources: [Cedars-Sinai](https://www.cedars-sinai.org/content/dam/cedars-sinai/blog/2017/05/pollen-guide-in-southern-california.pdf) (common sources); [UCLA Health](https://www.uclahealth.org/news/rain-allergies-kids-pollen) (trees), the [AAFA](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/aafa-2026-allergy-capitals-report.pdf) (grass), USC allergist Dr. Shijun Cindy Xi as reported by [LAist](https://laist.com/shows/take-two/socals-allergy-season-is-never-ending-and-could-get-worse) (weeds), and the [National Weather Service](https://www.weather.gov/safety/wind-mountain-valley) and [NOAA](https://www.climate.gov/news-features/event-tracker/weather-and-climate-influences-january-2025-fires-around-los-angeles) (Santa Ana winds).*

## Winter and spring allergies in Los Angeles: tree pollen

Tree pollen is most prevalent in Southern California from January through May, [UCLA Health](https://www.uclahealth.org/news/rain-allergies-kids-pollen) says. Cedars-Sinai notes that tree pollen is fine and powdery and can travel for miles on the wind.

Light rain can clear pollen from the air, UCLA pediatric allergist Dr. Maria Garcia-Lloret explains, but heavy storms can break pollen into smaller particles that are easier to inhale and reach deeper into the lungs. She suggests extra care for three to five days after heavy rain. Our [tree pollen allergy guide](/blog/tree-pollen-allergy) covers symptoms and treatment.

## Summer allergies in Los Angeles: grass pollen

Grass causes most late spring and summer allergy symptoms, usually from April through early June, the [AAFA](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/aafa-2026-allergy-capitals-report.pdf) says, and grass pollen can be found year-round in warmer parts of the country.

According to [Cedars-Sinai](https://www.cedars-sinai.org/content/dam/cedars-sinai/blog/2017/05/pollen-guide-in-southern-california.pdf), most grasses release pollen only when they grow tall, and perennial ryegrass and tall fescue usually don't flower until they're about 12 inches high. Bermuda grass is the exception: it can release pollen even when it's kept short, so a mowed Bermuda lawn can still put pollen in the air.

## Fall allergies in Los Angeles: weeds

"Weeds are really summer and early fall," Dr. Shijun Cindy Xi of USC's Keck School of Medicine [told LAist](https://laist.com/shows/take-two/socals-allergy-season-is-never-ending-and-could-get-worse). Cedars-Sinai's list for Southern California includes sagebrush, western ragweed, wormwood, amaranth and black mustard, along with coyotebrush and silverscale. Our [ragweed allergy guide](/blog/ragweed-allergy) covers ragweed and its relatives.

## Santa Ana winds and allergies

Santa Ana winds are strong, dry winds that flow from high pressure over the desert Southwest, east to west through Southern California's mountain passes toward the coast. The [National Weather Service](https://www.weather.gov/safety/wind-mountain-valley) explains that they typically feel warm or even hot, because the air compresses and heats up as it comes down the mountains, and says they're most common from September through May, and [NOAA's Climate.gov](https://www.climate.gov/news-features/event-tracker/weather-and-climate-influences-january-2025-fires-around-los-angeles) puts their peak in December and January. They also raise wildfire risk.

During one Santa Ana event, when the pollen count was zero, UCLA allergist Dr. Rita Kachru [told LAist](https://laist.com/news/santa-ana-winds-allergies) that the sneezing wasn't allergies. LAist reported wildfire ash, other particles and mold spores as the likely culprits. Dr. Kachru said those small particles get into your nose and eyes and cause an inflammatory reaction rather than a true allergy. She recommended rinsing your sinuses and eyes after time outside, and said antihistamines and decongestants can help once symptoms start.

The [South Coast AQMD](https://www.aqmd.gov/home/air-quality/wildfire-health-info-smoke-tips) lists people with lung diseases such as asthma among those most vulnerable to wildfire smoke. On smoky days it advises staying inside with windows and doors closed, running the air conditioner with a clean filter and the fresh air intake closed, and wearing a well fitted N95 or P100 respirator if you have to go out. It says not to rely on cloth or surgical masks for protection, and that respirators help only when they're worn properly and fit tightly.

## Why are my allergies so bad in Los Angeles?

Tree pollens peak from January through May, grass can linger all year, weeds carry into fall, and Santa Ana winds blow from September through May, so the seasons overlap and barely stop. A person allergic to more than one of these can have symptoms in nearly every month.

Wet and dry years cause different problems. Heavy storms can break pollen into finer particles that reach deeper into the lungs, [UCLA Health](https://www.uclahealth.org/news/rain-allergies-kids-pollen) says, while dry, windy weeks bring ash, smoke and other irritants.

## How to cut your exposure in Los Angeles

**1. Close windows in the afternoon.** The [AAFA](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/aafa-2026-allergy-capitals-report.pdf) recommends keeping windows closed during pollen season or peak times, usually the afternoon, and running central air or a HEPA air cleaner if you have one.

**2. Rinse off after time outside.** Shower before bed so pollen doesn't end up on your pillow, the AAFA advises. UCLA Health adds washing your hands after being outdoors and trying a saline nasal rinse.

**3. Take care for a few days after heavy rain.** Dr. Garcia-Lloret suggests extra precautions for three to five days after a big storm, when broken up pollen particles are in the air.

**4. Seal up on smoke days.** Keep doors and windows shut, set the air conditioner to recirculate, and use filters rated MERV 13 or higher, as the [South Coast AQMD](https://www.aqmd.gov/home/air-quality/wildfire-health-info-smoke-tips) recommends. A portable HEPA air cleaner can give you one room with filtered air, such as a bedroom.

## Longer term options for Los Angeles allergies

Allergy medicine controls symptoms while you take it, but it doesn't change the allergy. Allergy nasal sprays can take a week or two of regular use to work, Dr. Xi said, and work best when started before the season. If medicine isn't enough, immunotherapy builds tolerance to an allergen over time, and it comes in three forms.

**Allergy shots (SCIT)** are given in a health care setting, usually the allergist's office. The [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/allergy-shots-%28immunotherapy%29) describes shots once or twice a week for four to six months during build-up, then every two to four weeks for three to five years. Our [guide to allergy shots](/learn-about-ilit/allergy-shots-and-allergy-immunotherapy) explains the schedule.

**Allergy drops and tablets (SLIT)** dissolve under the tongue at home. Our [comparison of allergy shots, allergy drops and ILIT](/learn-about-ilit/allergy-shots-vs-allergy-drops-vs-ilit) covers which allergens each one treats.

**ILIT** is the shortest course, and for some people a faster alternative to allergy shots. An allergist injects FDA approved allergen extract into a lymph node at the top of the thigh, near the hip, guided by ultrasound. Giving the extract that way is an off label use. A typical course is about three injections over about two months, the schedule used in a [2008 trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2582048/) that compared it with three years of shots for grass pollen allergy. ILIT research is promising but smaller than the research behind allergy shots, and results vary from person to person. Our [ILIT FDA FAQ](/learn-about-ilit#faq-fda) covers the FDA question in more detail.
