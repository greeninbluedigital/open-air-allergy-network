KEY TAKEAWAYS
- Puffy, swollen eyelids are a common allergy symptom, and allergic puffiness is often worst first thing in the morning.
- Eye allergies usually affect both eyes and itch. Swelling in one eye, with pain, a fever or vision changes, needs a provider.
- A cool compress and lubricating eye drops are safe first steps, and antihistamine eye drops ease the itch within about an hour.
- Redness relief drops are for two or three days at most, because overuse brings the redness back.
- Swollen eyelids along with trouble breathing or a swollen tongue are an emergency. Call 911.

FAQS
Q: How long do swollen eyes from allergies last?
A: Anywhere from under an hour to months. [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/allergic-conjunctivitis) says allergic conjunctivitis symptoms can fade in less than an hour or last days, weeks or months, and [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/001031.htm) notes they can keep going as long as you're exposed. Sudden allergic swelling of the eyelids (angioedema) usually lasts a few hours to a couple of days, and Cleveland Clinic says providers look for other causes when it lasts longer than two or three days.

Q: Can allergies cause swelling in just one eye?
A: It's less typical. The [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/eye-symptom) and the [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/conjunctivitis/signs-symptoms/index.html) both say eye allergies usually affect both eyes, while viral pink eye usually starts in one eye and spreads to the other within days. An infection of the eyelid can also swell one side, so a single puffy eye that's red, painful or comes with a fever is worth getting checked ([MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/000976.htm)).

Q: Does Benadryl help swollen eyes from allergies?
A: It can, but it often makes people sleepy. [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/allergic-conjunctivitis) says diphenhydramine (Benadryl) can help with allergic conjunctivitis, and that allergists recommend newer, second generation antihistamines instead. The [ACAAI](https://acaai.org/allergies/allergic-conditions/eye-allergy/) adds that allergy pills can ease eye itching but may dry the eyes and make eye symptoms worse, so antihistamine eye drops are often the more direct fix.

Q: What are allergic shiners?
A: Dark circles under the eyes caused by allergies. The [ACAAI](https://acaai.org/allergies/allergic-conditions/eye-allergy/) says seasonal eye allergies can bring puffy eyelids along with these dark circles, and [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/allergic-conjunctivitis) lists allergic shiners among allergic conjunctivitis symptoms.

Q: Is allergic conjunctivitis contagious?
A: No. The [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/eye-symptom) and [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/allergic-conjunctivitis) both say eye allergies aren't contagious, unlike pink eye caused by a virus or bacteria, which the [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/conjunctivitis/signs-symptoms/index.html) calls very contagious. If you can't tell which one you have, a provider can check, and that matters because the treatments differ.

Q: Can food allergies cause swollen eyes?
A: Yes. [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/22632-angioedema) says allergies to foods can trigger angioedema, swelling under the skin that most often hits the lips and eyelids. The [ACAAI](https://acaai.org/allergies/allergic-conditions/food/) says most food allergy symptoms start within minutes to two hours of eating. Swelling with hives, a swollen tongue or trouble breathing calls for epinephrine and 911.

Q: What eye drops help swollen eyes from allergies?
A: Antihistamine eye drops are the usual choice. [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/allergic-conjunctivitis) lists over the counter and prescription drops with ketotifen, bepotastine, azelastine or cetirizine. The [ACAAI](https://acaai.org/allergies/allergic-conditions/eye-allergy/) says antihistamine drops can reduce itching, redness and swelling, though the effect may last only a few hours. Mast cell stabilizer drops prevent symptoms if you start them before you're exposed. A pharmacist can help you choose among the over the counter drops, and if those aren't enough, a doctor or allergist can prescribe something stronger.

Q: Is there a fast allergy treatment for eye allergies?
A: Antihistamine eye drops and a cool compress work within the hour, but they wear off. Immunotherapy treats the allergy behind the swelling, and the [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/eye-symptom) calls it a proven approach for eye allergies. ILIT allergy treatment (intralymphatic immunotherapy) is the shortest course, three injections over two months in the [2008 grass pollen trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2582048/), compared with three to five years of allergy shots. To ask about it, use [Find a Provider](/find-an-ilit-provider) on OpenAirAllergyNetwork.com to locate an allergist who offers it.

Yes, allergies can cause puffy eyes. Puffy, swollen eyelids are a standard hay fever symptom on the American College of Allergy, Asthma and Immunology's ([ACAAI](https://acaai.org/allergies/allergic-conditions/hay-fever/)) list, and [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/001031.htm) says allergic puffy eyelids show up most often in the morning. Allergy swelling usually comes with itching, redness and watery eyes in both eyes. A cool compress and antihistamine eye drops are the usual first steps. Swelling in one eye with pain or a fever, or any swelling with trouble breathing, needs care right away.

## When to get care right away

Call 911 if eyelid or face swelling comes with trouble breathing, wheezing, a swollen tongue or a very hoarse, whispery voice. [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/000844.htm) lists these as signs of anaphylaxis, a severe allergic reaction, and says to use epinephrine right away if you have it.

Contact a provider right away if a swollen eye is painful or hard to move, looks like it's bulging, or comes with a fever or vision changes. MedlinePlus lists those as warning signs of an [infection around the eye](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/000976.htm), which is treated with antibiotics. [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/21930-eyelid-dermatitis) adds that eyelid swelling so severe you can't see needs emergency care.

## Why allergies make your eyes puffy

When you're allergic, your immune system overreacts to something harmless and releases histamine. The [ACAAI](https://acaai.org/allergies/allergic-conditions/eye-allergy/) explains that histamine makes tiny blood vessels leak, and the result is itchy, red, watery and puffy eyes. Doctors call it allergic conjunctivitis, and [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/allergic-conjunctivitis) says it may affect up to 40% of people at some point.

The ACAAI lists pollen from grass, trees and weeds as the outdoor triggers, and pet dander, dust mites and mold indoors. Cleveland Clinic adds chemicals and fragrances in soaps, deodorants, moisturizers and perfume.

Eyelid dermatitis also swells the eyelids, and it starts in the skin of the lid itself, which reacts to something it touches. [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/21930-eyelid-dermatitis) says cosmetics are a common cause, along with things like false eyelashes, hair dye and even eye drops. The lids turn red and scaly and may swell, usually a day or two after you use the product.

Angioedema is sudden swelling under the skin, and [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/22632-angioedema) says it most often hits the lips and eyelids. Allergies to foods, medicines, latex or insect stings can set it off. Prescription drugs called ACE inhibitors can cause it without any allergy, and the swelling can start weeks or months after you begin taking one, so mention any new prescriptions to your doctor.

## Allergy puffy eyes in the morning

MedlinePlus lists puffy eyelids, "most often in the morning," among allergic conjunctivitis symptoms. If you wake up puffy most days, start with the bedroom. The ACAAI names dust mites, pet dander and mold as the causes of year round eye allergies, and suggests mite proof covers for pillows and mattresses and washing bedding in hot water. Our [dust mite allergy guide](/blog/dust-mite-allergy) walks through the bedroom steps. Dog and cat owners can start with our guides to [dog allergy signs](/blog/allergic-to-my-dog) and [how long cat allergy symptoms last](/blog/how-long-do-cat-allergy-symptoms-last).

MedlinePlus says symptoms may be worse when there's more pollen in the air, which is more likely on hot, dry, windy days.

Viral and bacterial pink eye spread easily, the CDC says. Allergies are one of the common causes of blepharitis, according to Cleveland Clinic, so the two can show up together.

## How to get rid of puffy eyes from allergies fast

1. **Hold a cool compress over your closed eyes.** [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/001031.htm) and [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/allergic-conjunctivitis) both recommend it.
2. **Don't rub.** The [ACAAI](https://acaai.org/allergies/allergic-conditions/eye-allergy/) warns that rubbing itchy eyes can make symptoms worse and may lead to an infection.
3. **Rinse and soothe.** Cleveland Clinic suggests flushing your eyes with water. The ACAAI says artificial tears are safe to use as often as you need.
4. **Use antihistamine eye drops.** Cleveland Clinic says they come over the counter as well as by prescription, and a pharmacist can help you choose one. The ACAAI says they can reduce itching, redness and swelling, though the effect may last only a few hours, and Cleveland Clinic says eye drops start working in about an hour. Take out contact lenses first, since MedlinePlus says not to use eye drops while wearing them. If over-the-counter drops don't help, the ACAAI recommends seeing an allergist.
5. **Go easy on redness relief drops.** Decongestant drops shrink redness, but the ACAAI says to use them no more than two or three days, because overuse brings the redness back. Skip them if you have glaucoma.
6. **Add a non-drowsy allergy pill if your nose is acting up too.** Cleveland Clinic lists fexofenadine, loratadine and cetirizine, and says these work in about 30 minutes. For the eyes alone, drops are the better fit, since the ACAAI says pills may dry the eyes and make eye symptoms worse.
7. **Keep the allergen away from your face.** The ACAAI suggests glasses outdoors, closed windows with air conditioning during pollen season, and washing your hands after touching pets. If your eyelid skin is red and scaly, Cleveland Clinic says to skip eye makeup until it heals.

## When to see a doctor or allergist about swollen eyes

[MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/001031.htm) says to contact a provider if home care and over-the-counter medicine aren't helping, your vision changes, eye pain is severe or getting worse, or you have a headache along with your eye symptoms. The [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/conjunctivitis/signs-symptoms/index.html) adds light sensitivity, blurred vision and intense redness. If your eyes puff up every spring or every time you visit a friend with a cat, an allergist can find the trigger. MedlinePlus says skin testing is the most common way, and our article on [skin tests vs. blood tests](/blog/allergy-skin-test-vs-blood-test) explains both.

For stubborn cases, an allergist may prescribe stronger drops. Steroid eye drops treat severe symptoms, but the ACAAI says using them longer than two weeks needs an eye doctor's supervision because of glaucoma and cataract risks.

## Longer term options for eye allergies

**Allergy medicine**, meaning eye drops and allergy pills, controls symptoms while you use it. It doesn't change the allergy.

**Allergy shots** are one form of immunotherapy, which the [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/eye-symptom) calls a proven approach for eye allergies. Its [allergy shots page](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/allergy-shots-%28immunotherapy%29) describes shots once or twice a week for four to six months, then every two to four weeks for three to five years.

**Allergy tablets** dissolve under the tongue at home. The [ACAAI](https://acaai.org/allergies/allergic-conditions/hay-fever/) notes they're available only for certain grass and ragweed pollens and house dust mites.

**ILIT (intralymphatic immunotherapy)** is the shortest immunotherapy course, and for some people a faster alternative to allergy shots. An allergist injects FDA approved allergen extract into a lymph node in the groin (just below the waist, near the hip), an [off label use](/learn-about-ilit#faq-fda) that our Learn About ILIT page explains. The [2008 trial that established ILIT](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2582048/) enrolled 165 people with grass pollen rhinoconjunctivitis, meaning allergy symptoms of both the nose and the eyes. It gave three injections over two months, compared with 54 shots over three years. The research is promising but smaller than for allergy shots, and results vary from person to person.

Our [comparison of allergy shots, allergy drops and ILIT](/learn-about-ilit/allergy-shots-vs-allergy-drops-vs-ilit) lays out the options side by side. If puffy, itchy eyes come back every allergy season, talk with an allergist now about treating the allergy itself.
