KEY TAKEAWAYS
- Michigan's pollen year runs from tree pollen as early as February to ragweed, which lasts until the first frost in September or October.
- May is often the hardest month, because tree and grass pollen are in the air together.
- Grand Rapids ranked 54th and Detroit 58th of the 100 largest U.S. metros in the AAFA's 2026 Allergy Capitals report, both rated average overall.
- Michigan has no National Allergy Bureau pollen counting station, so local pollen numbers are often estimates based on other states or past patterns.
- Start allergy medicine a couple of weeks before your allergy season begins. If medicine isn't enough, immunotherapy treats the allergy itself, and ILIT is the shortest course, typically about three injections over about two months.

FAQS
Q: When does allergy season start in Michigan?
A: For people allergic to tree pollen, it can start as early as February, according to [Blue Cross Blue Shield of Michigan](https://www.bcbsm.mibluedaily.com/stories/prevention/when-is-allergy-season), which names trees as the top allergy culprit in the state. That's later than parts of the South, where the [AAFA](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/aafa-2026-allergy-capitals-report.pdf) says trees can release pollen as early as December or January.

Q: When is peak allergy season in Michigan?
A: May is often the worst month, says [Blue Cross Blue Shield of Michigan](https://www.bcbsm.mibluedaily.com/stories/prevention/when-is-allergy-season), because tree pollen is still in the air when grass pollen starts. People allergic to ragweed have a second peak in fall. The [AAFA](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/aafa-2026-allergy-capitals-report.pdf) says ragweed peaks in mid September in most of the U.S.

Q: When is allergy season over in Michigan?
A: Pollen season usually ends with the first frost, which can come in September or October depending on where you live in Michigan, according to [Blue Cross Blue Shield of Michigan](https://www.bcbsm.mibluedaily.com/stories/prevention/when-is-allergy-season). The [AAFA](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/aafa-2026-allergy-capitals-report.pdf) notes that the first frost is coming later as temperatures warm, which keeps weeds growing longer.

Q: Why are allergies so bad in Michigan?
A: Michigan isn't among the worst places nationally. In the [AAFA's 2026 Allergy Capitals report](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/aafa-2026-allergy-capitals-report.pdf), Grand Rapids and Detroit both rated average overall. But the AAFA says ragweed populations are especially high in Midwestern states, and warmer years bring earlier springs and later frosts, so the pollen year is getting longer.

Q: When is ragweed season in Michigan?
A: Ragweed starts in August, according to [Blue Cross Blue Shield of Michigan](https://www.bcbsm.mibluedaily.com/stories/prevention/when-is-allergy-season), and lasts until the first frost. The [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/ragweed) says that in many parts of the country, ragweed symptoms now begin in early August and run through October. Our [ragweed allergy guide](/blog/ragweed-allergy) covers symptoms and treatment.

Q: Are there winter allergies in Michigan?
A: Yes, though pollen usually isn't the cause once the frost has come. The [AAFA](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/aafa-2026-allergy-capitals-report.pdf) says the same nose allergy symptoms can come from mold, dust mites and animal dander, and that in some places mold persists year round. The [ACAAI](https://acaai.org/news/do-you-have-the-winter-sniffles-5-ways-you-can-manage-indoor-winter-allergies/) says indoor allergies are noticed more in winter, when you spend more time at home with the windows shut. Our [dust mite allergy guide](/blog/dust-mite-allergy) covers the most common indoor trigger.

Q: Where can I find the Grand Rapids pollen count?
A: Michigan doesn't have a National Allergy Bureau counting station, University of Michigan researcher Allison Steiner told [FOX 17](https://www.fox17online.com/news/local-news/michigan/breaking-down-the-blind-spots-of-pollen-counting-as-we-kick-off-allergy-season). Without one, FOX 17 reports, Michiganders are left with estimates based on other states or past patterns. The [AAFA](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/aafa-2026-allergy-capitals-report.pdf) says a pollen count taken from samples at a monitoring site or sensor is more accurate than a forecast, and that some apps show counts from automated sensors in your area.

Q: Is there a faster alternative to allergy shots in Michigan?
A: Yes. ILIT (intralymphatic immunotherapy) is typically about three injections over about two months, compared with three to five years of maintenance shots described by the [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/allergy-shots-%28immunotherapy%29). The research is promising but smaller than for allergy shots. To try it, use [Find a Provider](/find-an-ilit-provider) on OpenAirAllergyNetwork.com to locate an allergist who offers it.

Michigan allergy season starts with tree pollen in late winter or early spring, moves to grass in late spring, and ends with ragweed, which keeps going until the first frost in September or October. For people allergic to all three, that's most of the year from February to fall, and May is often the roughest month, when trees and grass overlap.

Whichever pollen gets to you, knowing your month helps you start medicine on time and tell an allergist exactly when symptoms hit. That matters if you're thinking about treating the allergy itself with allergy shots, allergy drops, or ILIT, a faster alternative to allergy shots. Below you'll find a month by month table, what each season brings, how Michigan's cities rank, and what helps.

[Tree Pollen Allergy: Symptoms, Season and Treatment](/blog/tree-pollen-allergy "card")

| Month | What's in the air | What to know |
|---|---|---|
| January | Indoor allergens | Pollen season hasn't started. Indoor allergies are noticed more in winter, with more time at home, windows shut and the heat recirculating the air |
| February | Indoor allergens, early tree pollen | Some trees can start this early, while the house is still closed up for winter |
| March | Tree pollen, indoor allergens | Trees are Michigan's top allergy culprit. Indoor triggers linger while the windows stay shut |
| April | Tree pollen | Grass starts in April |
| May | Tree and grass pollen | Often the worst month, because the two overlap |
| June | Grass pollen | Grass season usually winds down by the end of June |
| July | Grass pollen, outdoor mold | Grass can linger into summer. Mold spores rise around storms |
| August | Ragweed begins | Ragweed and other weeds start this month |
| September | Ragweed | Ragweed peaks in mid September in most areas |
| October | Ragweed until the first frost | The first frost can come in September or October, depending on where you live in Michigan |

*Sources: Michigan timing from [Blue Cross Blue Shield of Michigan](https://www.bcbsm.mibluedaily.com/stories/prevention/when-is-allergy-season) (medically reviewed), national timing and mold from the [AAFA](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/aafa-2026-allergy-capitals-report.pdf) and the [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/ragweed), and winter indoor allergies from the [ACAAI](https://acaai.org/news/do-you-have-the-winter-sniffles-5-ways-you-can-manage-indoor-winter-allergies/).*

A University of Michigan team describing the state's pollen in a [2025 study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11737261/) lays out the same order: deciduous and evergreen trees in spring, grass most common from late spring into early summer, and ragweed highest in fall.

## Spring allergies in Michigan: tree pollen

[Blue Cross Blue Shield of Michigan](https://www.bcbsm.mibluedaily.com/stories/prevention/when-is-allergy-season) says trees can start releasing pollen as early as February and keep going through late spring, depending on the species, and calls trees the top allergy culprit in Michigan. Michigan's start is later than parts of the South, where the [AAFA](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/aafa-2026-allergy-capitals-report.pdf) says trees can release pollen as early as December or January. Near Lake Michigan, the lake itself can hold spring back: the [Detroit Free Press](https://www.freep.com/story/news/local/michigan/2022/01/30/climate-change-michigan-fruit-crop-cherries-apples-grapes/6636203001/) reported that westerly winds crossing the cold water as winter turns to spring keep fruit trees just east of the lake dormant a little longer, because the lake takes so much energy to warm.

Maple, oak, birch, ash, elm, box elder and cottonwood are all on the AAFA's list of trees that cause the most allergy symptoms in the U.S. The pollen that makes you sneeze is too small to see. The AAFA explains that the yellow dust you see coating outdoor surfaces is heavy and falls near the tree, while the light pollen that rides the wind is what most people react to.

If raw apples, cherries, peaches or pears make your mouth itch or tingle, birch pollen may be the link. The AAFA lists those fruits among the foods that can cause oral allergy syndrome in people with a birch pollen allergy. Our [oral allergy syndrome guide](/blog/oral-allergy-syndrome) has the full list.

## Summer allergies in Michigan: grass pollen

Grass pollen starts in April and runs through June in Michigan, according to Blue Cross Blue Shield of Michigan, and the [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/mowing-down-your-grass-allergies) says grass usually pollinates from April through early June across most of the country. Timothy, Kentucky blue, rye and fescue are among the common northern grasses the AAAAI names.

Because trees are still pollinating when grass starts, May is often the worst month for Michigan allergy sufferers. Grass pollen is light and carried by the wind, the AAFA says, so you can still run into it even if you aren't allergic to the grass near your home.

## Fall allergies in Michigan: ragweed until the first frost

Ragweed is Michigan's main fall trigger, and the [AAFA](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/aafa-2026-allergy-capitals-report.pdf) calls it the worst of the weed pollens, says about 1 in 7 people are allergic to it, and reports that ragweed populations are particularly high in Midwestern and Eastern states. In Michigan it starts in August and lasts until the first frost, which Blue Cross Blue Shield of Michigan says may come in September or October depending on where you live.

The AAFA puts the ragweed peak at mid September in most areas. The [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/ragweed) notes that in many parts of the country, symptoms now begin in early August and run through October. Our [ragweed allergy guide](/blog/ragweed-allergy) covers symptoms and treatment.

## Mold and winter allergies in Michigan

Outdoor mold follows the weather: the AAFA says airborne mold spores climb before, during and after storms, and that research shows the Midwest is seeing shifts in how long and how intense mold season is. Milder winters may also mean more days with mold in the air. In fall, the AAFA suggests clearing leaves and piles of dead plants as soon as you can.

Once the frost ends pollen season, symptoms that hang on may have another cause. The AAFA says the same nose allergy can come from mold, dust mites and animal dander. Those indoor triggers are around all year, but the [American College of Allergy, Asthma and Immunology](https://acaai.org/news/do-you-have-the-winter-sniffles-5-ways-you-can-manage-indoor-winter-allergies/) says you notice them more in winter because you spend more time at home with the doors and windows shut, sealing allergens in, while the heating system recirculates indoor air and any allergy triggers in it. Our guide to [cleaning your home to reduce allergies](/blog/how-to-clean-house-for-allergies) covers those triggers room by room.

Grand Rapids and Detroit are the two Michigan metros on the list.

## How to get through a Michigan allergy season

These steps follow the [AAFA's 2026 report](https://aafa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/aafa-2026-allergy-capitals-report.pdf), matched to Michigan's calendar.

1. **Start medicine before your month.** The AAFA suggests starting allergy medicine a couple of weeks before your season begins. With trees starting as early as February, a tree allergy can mean starting in late winter. Nasal steroid sprays are the most effective type for nose symptoms, and many are sold over the counter.
2. **Keep windows closed in your peak months.** That means May for tree and grass allergies and September for ragweed. The AAFA says peak pollen times are usually in the afternoon. Use central air or an air cleaner with a HEPA filter if you have one.
3. **Leave pollen at the door.** Take off your shoes, change and wash clothes after time outdoors, and dry laundry in the dryer or on an indoor rack rather than a line.
4. **Shower before bed.** It washes pollen off your body so it doesn't end up in your bedding.
5. **Clear wet leaves in fall and keep indoor humidity down.** The AAFA suggests keeping indoor humidity between 30% and 50%, checked with a hygrometer, and running a dehumidifier if needed. Cleaning gutters and directing water away from the house helps too.

## Allergy testing in Michigan

Tree, grass and ragweed pollen cause the same sneezing and itchy eyes, so the calendar can only narrow it down. The AAFA says having your allergies evaluated by an allergist is important, partly because mold allergy is managed differently from pollen allergy. A skin test or blood test can show which allergens you react to. Our guide to [allergy skin tests vs. blood tests](/blog/allergy-skin-test-vs-blood-test) explains the options.

## Longer term options for Michigan allergies

Allergy medicine eases symptoms while you take it. If it doesn't give you complete relief, the AAFA suggests talking with an allergist about immunotherapy, which can change how your immune system responds to an allergen.

**Allergy shots (SCIT)** are the longest course. The [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/allergy-shots-%28immunotherapy%29) describes shots once or twice a week for four to six months, then every two to four weeks for three to five years. Our [guide to allergy shots](/learn-about-ilit/allergy-shots-and-allergy-immunotherapy) walks through the schedule.

**Allergy tablets and drops (SLIT)** go under the tongue. The [AAAAI](https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/mowing-down-your-grass-allergies) says a daily grass pollen tablet is approved in the U.S., and it lists tablets as a ragweed option too. Our [comparison of allergy shots, allergy drops and ILIT](/learn-about-ilit/allergy-shots-vs-allergy-drops-vs-ilit) covers which allergens each one treats.

**ILIT (intralymphatic immunotherapy)** is the shortest course, and for some people a faster alternative to allergy shots. An allergist uses ultrasound to inject FDA approved allergen extract into a lymph node, usually in the groin (just below the waist, near the hip). Giving the extract that way is an off label use. A typical course is about three injections over about two months, the schedule from a [2008 trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2582048/) in people with grass pollen allergy. In that study, tolerance to grass pollen in the nose rose within four months, and after three years it was comparable to three years of allergy shots, with fewer allergic side effects.

ILIT research is promising but smaller than the research behind allergy shots, and results vary from person to person. The [ILIT FDA FAQ](/learn-about-ilit#faq-fda) explains the off label question.
